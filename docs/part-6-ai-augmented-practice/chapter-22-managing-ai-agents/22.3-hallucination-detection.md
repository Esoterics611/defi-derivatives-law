# 22.3 Hallucination Detection in Legal AI

!!! info "Section Objectives"
    After completing this section, you will be able to:

    - Classify AI hallucinations into the five legal-specific categories
    - Apply detection strategies for each hallucination type
    - Build automated verification pipelines using multi-agent architectures
    - Implement the 5-step citation verification workflow

---

## The Hallucination Taxonomy for Legal AI

Not all AI errors are the same. Legal hallucinations fall into five distinct categories, each with different detection methods, prevention strategies, and risk profiles:

### 1. Fabricated Cases

The classic failure mode. The AI generates a case citation that does not exist — a plausible case name, a real-sounding reporter citation, but no actual case behind it.

**Example**: "In *CFTC v. Meridian Digital Holdings*, No. 22-cv-1847 (S.D.N.Y. 2023), the court held that automated market makers constitute swap execution facilities under CEA §5h." — This case does not exist.

**Why it happens**: Language models predict likely text sequences. Legal citations follow predictable patterns (agency abbreviation + "v." + entity name + docket format + court + year). The model generates a pattern-matching citation rather than retrieving a real one.

### 2. Misattributed Holdings

The case is real, but the AI attributes a holding that the case does not actually establish.

**Example**: Citing *CFTC v. McDonnell* (E.D.N.Y. 2018) for the proposition that "all digital assets are commodities under the CEA" — the case actually held that virtual currencies are commodities, a narrower holding that does not extend to all digital assets.

**Why it happens**: The model has seen the case cited in many contexts and conflates the various propositions it has been cited for with what the case actually held.

### 3. Outdated Law

The AI cites a statute, rule, or case that has been superseded, amended, or overruled.

**Example**: Citing the pre-2012 swap dealer de minimis threshold of $8 billion when the CFTC phased in a permanent threshold. Or citing a CFTC no-action letter that has since expired.

**Why it happens**: Training data includes legal text from all time periods. Without explicit temporal context, the model may retrieve and present outdated authorities.

### 4. Jurisdictional Confusion

The AI applies legal standards from the wrong jurisdiction — UK Financial Conduct Authority rules when asked about US CFTC requirements, or state law when federal law governs.

**Example**: Describing "best execution" obligations for swap dealers when CFTC rules do not impose a best execution standard equivalent to the UK's FCA COBS 11.2 requirements.

**Why it happens**: Derivatives regulation exists in every major jurisdiction, with similar concepts but different rules. The model conflates jurisdictions with overlapping terminology.

### 5. Regulatory Version Errors

The AI cites the wrong rule number, Part, or section after CFTC rule reorganizations, renumberings, or amendments.

**Example**: Citing "17 CFR § 23.431" for swap dealer business conduct when a rule reorganization moved requirements to a different section, or citing rule text that reflects an earlier version of the regulation.

**Why it happens**: CFTC rules have been reorganized multiple times, especially after Dodd-Frank implementation. The model may mix rule numbers from different organizational schemes.

## Hallucination Risk Matrix

| Type | Frequency | Detection Method | Prevention Strategy | Risk If Undetected |
|------|-----------|-----------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Fabricated cases** | High | Westlaw/Lexis cross-check | Instruct AI to cite statutes over cases; require docket numbers | Citing non-existent authority in a filing or memo — professional liability, credibility loss |
| **Misattributed holdings** | Medium-High | Read the actual case | Ask AI to quote the specific holding language | Misstating law — incorrect legal advice, wrong compliance conclusions |
| **Outdated law** | Medium | Check current version on eCFR.gov | Set date context in every prompt; ask AI to flag uncertainty about currency | Relying on superseded authority — compliance gaps, missed obligations |
| **Jurisdictional confusion** | Medium | Verify jurisdiction in each cited authority | Specify jurisdiction explicitly in system prompt; restrict to named authorities | Applying wrong legal standard — compliance program based on inapplicable rules |
| **Regulatory version errors** | Low-Medium | Cross-reference on eCFR.gov | Provide current CFR version in prompt context | Citing wrong rule — minor if caught early, serious if embedded in compliance systems |

## Detection Strategies

### Citation Verification Workflows

Every AI-generated legal citation must pass through a verification step before it is relied upon:

**Westlaw/LexisNexis Cross-Check**: For any case citation, search the case name and docket number in a legal database. If it doesn't appear, it doesn't exist.

**eCFR.gov Verification**: For any regulatory citation (17 CFR §XX.XXX), check the current text on eCFR.gov. Compare the AI's description of the rule against the actual text.

**Federal Register Verification**: For proposed rules, final rules, and no-action letters, verify in the Federal Register or on CFTC.gov/enforcement.

### The "Doubt Trigger" Heuristic

Develop an instinct for when to verify:

- **You've never heard of the case** → verify it exists
- **The holding seems too perfectly on point** → verify the holding
- **The rule number seems unfamiliar** → check eCFR.gov
- **The analysis reaches a surprising conclusion** → trace each step of the reasoning
- **The AI expresses no uncertainty** → be more suspicious, not less

### Confidence Calibration

Ask the AI to rate its own certainty — then treat the ratings as information, not as truth:

```
For each legal citation in your analysis:
1. Rate your confidence that this authority exists: Certain / Likely / Uncertain
2. Rate your confidence that you have correctly stated the holding or rule text:
   Certain / Likely / Uncertain
3. If "Uncertain" on either dimension, flag the citation for human verification
```

AI self-confidence ratings are imperfect — models are sometimes confident about fabricated citations. But the "Uncertain" flags are useful: when the model expresses uncertainty, it is almost always warranted.

### Adversarial Prompting

Challenge the AI on suspicious citations:

```
You cited [case name]. I need to verify this citation.
1. What is the exact docket number?
2. Which court decided it?
3. What was the exact date of the decision?
4. What were the names of the parties?
5. Who was the presiding judge?
6. What reporter is it published in, and at what page?

If you are not certain about any of these details, say so explicitly.
```

If the AI cannot provide these details consistently, or if the details change when you ask again, the citation is likely fabricated.

## Building Automated Verification Pipelines

In a multi-agent architecture, verification becomes a distinct agent responsibility:

```
Generation Pipeline:
  Analysis Agent generates legal memo with citations
      ↓
  Verification Agent extracts all citations
      ↓
  For each citation:
    - Case citations → search legal database API
    - Rule citations → query eCFR API
    - Enforcement citations → search CFTC.gov
      ↓
  Verification Agent produces report:
    - Verified citations (confirmed to exist)
    - Unverifiable citations (not found — flag for human check)
    - Modified citations (exist but details differ — flag for review)
      ↓
  Human reviewer receives memo + verification report
```

This pipeline does not eliminate the need for human review. It **triages** — so the human reviewer can focus on the citations that failed automated verification rather than checking every citation manually.

!!! tip "AI Practitioner Note"
    **The 5-step citation verification workflow every AI-augmented legal professional must follow:**

    1. **Generate**: AI produces legal analysis with citations
    2. **Extract**: Pull every citation into a verification checklist — case names, rule numbers, statutory sections, enforcement orders
    3. **Automated check**: Run citations against available databases (eCFR for rules, CFTC.gov for enforcement actions, legal databases for cases)
    4. **Flag**: Mark each citation as Verified / Unverifiable / Discrepant
    5. **Human review**: Review all Unverifiable and Discrepant citations manually. For Verified citations, spot-check 10-20% to validate the automated process itself

    This workflow adds 15-30 minutes to an AI-generated legal analysis. That investment prevents the catastrophic credibility loss of citing a nonexistent case in a regulatory filing.
