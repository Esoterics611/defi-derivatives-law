# 17.1 CEA Anti-Manipulation Provisions

!!! info "Section Objectives"

    - Describe the broader fraud-based manipulation standard under CEA §6(c)(1) and Rule 180.1
    - Identify the wash trading and accommodation trading prohibitions under CEA §4c(a)
    - Analyze the Dodd-Frank expansion of manipulation authority
    - Assess the applicability of each provision to DeFi trading activity

## CEA §9(a)(2): Traditional Price Manipulation

The original anti-manipulation provision — CEA §9(a)(2) — makes it a felony to "manipulate or attempt to manipulate the price of any commodity in interstate commerce, or for future delivery on or subject to the rules of any registered entity." Conviction carries up to 25 years imprisonment and fines of up to $1 million for individuals.

The CFTC has historically applied a **four-part test** for price manipulation:

1. **Ability**: The defendant had the ability to influence market prices — sufficient market power, position size, or information advantage
2. **Specific intent**: The defendant acted with the specific intent to create an artificial price — not merely to profit, but to distort the market
3. **Artificial price**: Prices actually were artificial — they did not reflect legitimate forces of supply and demand
4. **Causation**: The defendant's actions caused the artificial price

This is a high bar. "Specific intent" is notoriously difficult to prove — the CFTC must demonstrate that the defendant intended to create an artificial price, not merely that they traded aggressively for profit. For decades, the specific intent requirement limited the CFTC's ability to prosecute manipulation, particularly in cases where the defendant's trading was facially legitimate but had manipulative effects.

| Element | What the CFTC Must Prove | Evidence Sources | DeFi Applicability |
|---------|-------------------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Ability | Market power, position dominance, information advantage | Trading data, position reports, market share analysis | On-chain position data, TVL concentration, governance token holdings |
| Specific intent | Purpose was to create artificial price, not mere profit | Communications, trading patterns, circumstantial evidence | On-chain transaction patterns, timing analysis, (pseudonymous — no communications) |
| Artificial price | Price deviated from legitimate supply/demand | Expert testimony, market analysis, counterfactual modeling | Oracle deviation analysis, cross-venue price comparison |
| Causation | Defendant's acts caused the artificial price | Temporal correlation, market impact analysis | Transaction ordering analysis, block-level causation |

## CEA §6(c)(1) / CFTC Rule 180.1: Fraud-Based Manipulation

Dodd-Frank Title VII added §6(c)(1) to the CEA, granting the CFTC authority to prohibit "any manipulative or deceptive device or contrivance" in connection with any swap, contract of sale of any commodity in interstate commerce, or contract for future delivery on a registered entity.

This provision — and its implementing rule, **CFTC Rule 180.1** — was explicitly modeled on SEC Rule 10b-5. The CFTC's intent was to import decades of SEC fraud jurisprudence into the derivatives context, creating a more flexible and prosecutable manipulation standard.

Rule 180.1 makes it unlawful to:

1. Use or employ any manipulative device, scheme, or artifice to defraud
2. Make any untrue statement of material fact or omit a material fact
3. Engage in any act, practice, or course of business that operates as a fraud or deceit

The critical difference from §9(a)(2): **Rule 180.1 does not require proof of specific intent to create an artificial price.** It requires only recklessness — a lower mental state that is far easier to prove. The CFTC can establish manipulation under Rule 180.1 by showing that the defendant employed a manipulative device recklessly, without proving that they specifically intended to distort prices.

This is the provision the CFTC used against Avraham Eisenberg in the Mango Markets case. It is also the provision most likely to be applied to DeFi manipulation going forward — its broad language and lower intent requirement make it a versatile enforcement tool.

!!! warning ""
    **The Rule 180.1 Advantage:** Before Dodd-Frank, the CFTC had to prove specific intent to manipulate — a near-impossible standard in many cases. Rule 180.1 requires only recklessness. This fundamentally changed the enforcement landscape. If you are engaging in aggressive trading strategies in DeFi that could affect prices, the relevant question is not "did I intend to manipulate?" but "would a reasonable person recognize that this strategy could be manipulative?" If the answer is yes, you have a Rule 180.1 problem.


## CEA §4c(a): Wash Trading & Fictitious Sales

CEA §4c(a) prohibits three related practices:

**Wash trading**: Entering into transactions that are "washed out" — the trader ends up in the same economic position as before. The classic example: simultaneously buying and selling the same futures contract through different accounts, creating the appearance of trading volume with no actual risk transfer.

**Accommodation trading**: Entering into transactions to assist another party in wash trading or manipulating prices. The "accommodating" party provides the other side of a fictitious transaction.

**Fictitious sales**: Any transaction used to report a price that is not a true and bona fide transaction price. Fictitious sales are designed to create false price signals in the market.

Section 4c(a) does not require proof of specific intent to manipulate prices — the prohibited conduct itself is the offense. If a transaction is a wash trade, it violates §4c(a) regardless of the trader's purpose. This makes §4c(a) particularly relevant to DeFi, where wash trading is rampant (airdrop farming, volume inflation, liquidity mining gaming) and intent is difficult to prove due to pseudonymity.

## The Dodd-Frank Expansion

Before Dodd-Frank, the CEA's anti-manipulation provisions applied primarily to futures and options on registered exchanges. Dodd-Frank expanded their reach to cover:

- **Swaps**: All swaps, whether cleared or uncleared, exchange-traded or OTC
- **Commodity transactions in interstate commerce**: Even spot commodity transactions, not just futures
- **Attempted manipulation**: §6(c)(3) makes attempted manipulation independently actionable — the CFTC doesn't need to prove that manipulation actually succeeded

This expansion is critical for DeFi. Many DeFi instruments are not futures (they don't trade on registered exchanges) but may be swaps or commodity transactions in interstate commerce. The Dodd-Frank expansion ensures that the CFTC's anti-manipulation authority reaches these instruments regardless of their exact classification.

| Provision | Scope | Mental State | Penalty (Civil) | Penalty (Criminal) |
|-----------|-------|-------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| §9(a)(2) | Price manipulation of any commodity | Specific intent | Treble damages for private action | Up to 25 years, $1M fine |
| §6(c)(1) / Rule 180.1 | Fraud-based manipulation in connection with any swap or commodity | Recklessness | Up to $1.4M per violation | Up to 25 years (if DOJ prosecutes) |
| §4c(a)(5)(C) | Spoofing | Intent to cancel | Up to $1.4M per violation | Up to 10 years, $1M fine |
| §4c(a) | Wash trading, accommodation trading, fictitious sales | No specific intent required — prohibited per se | Up to $1.4M per violation | Up to 10 years, $1M fine |
| §6(c)(3) | Attempted manipulation | Specific intent (but no success required) | Up to $1.4M per violation | Up to 25 years |

    **What to Verify:** AI models tend to overstate the strength of manipulation claims by assuming intent from trading patterns. Push back on the intent element — aggressive trading that moves prices is not automatically manipulative. The analysis must distinguish between legitimate (even if aggressive) trading and conduct that crosses the line into manipulation.

## Section Summary

- CEA §9(a)(2) is the traditional manipulation provision — powerful but requires proof of specific intent
- CEA §6(c)(1) / Rule 180.1 (post-Dodd-Frank) requires only recklessness — a far lower and more prosecutable standard
- CEA §4c(a) prohibits wash trading, accommodation trading, and fictitious sales without requiring specific intent
- Dodd-Frank expanded anti-manipulation authority to cover swaps, spot commodity transactions, and attempted manipulation
- For DeFi, Rule 180.1's recklessness standard and §4c(a)'s per se wash trading prohibition are the most significant enforcement tools
