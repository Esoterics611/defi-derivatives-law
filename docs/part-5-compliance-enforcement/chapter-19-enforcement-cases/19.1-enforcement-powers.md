# 19.1 CFTC Enforcement Powers

!!! info "Section Objectives"
    After completing this section, you will be able to:

    - Distinguish between administrative proceedings and federal court actions
    - Identify the CFTC's penalty powers including CMPs, disgorgement, restitution, and injunctions
    - Explain the criminal referral process to the DOJ
    - Describe the whistleblower program and its incentive structure
    - Assess how cooperation credit affects enforcement outcomes

---

## Enforcement Pathways

The CFTC pursues enforcement through two primary pathways:

**Administrative proceedings** are conducted before an Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) within the CFTC. The respondent receives a complaint, can contest the charges, and a hearing is held. The ALJ issues an initial decision, which can be appealed to the full Commission and then to a federal appellate court. Administrative proceedings are used for registered entities and individuals, and for cases where the CFTC seeks administrative penalties (fines, trading bans, registration revocations).

**Civil actions in federal court** are filed by the CFTC's Division of Enforcement in U.S. district court. Federal court actions allow the CFTC to seek broader remedies — including injunctions, asset freezes, and contempt sanctions — and are used for unregistered entities, fraud cases, and situations requiring emergency relief. Most DeFi enforcement actions have been federal court cases, because DeFi protocols are typically unregistered.

| Pathway | Forum | Burden of Proof | Remedies Available | Typical Use |
|---------|-------|----------------|-------------------|------------|
| Administrative | CFTC Administrative Law Judge | Preponderance of evidence | CMPs, cease and desist, trading bans, registration actions | Registered entities, compliance failures |
| Federal court (civil) | U.S. District Court | Preponderance of evidence | CMPs, disgorgement, restitution, injunctions, asset freezes | Unregistered entities, fraud, manipulation, emergency actions |
| Criminal referral (DOJ) | Federal criminal court | Beyond reasonable doubt | Imprisonment (up to 25 years), criminal fines, forfeiture | Manipulation, fraud, willful violations |

## Penalty Powers

**Civil monetary penalties (CMPs).** The CFTC can impose per-violation fines up to $190,132 (adjusted annually for inflation). For manipulation and fraud, penalties can be treble the monetary gain or the monetary loss caused by the violation. In major cases, aggregate CMPs reach tens or hundreds of millions of dollars.

**Disgorgement.** The CFTC can require respondents to surrender all profits derived from the violation. Disgorgement is not a penalty — it's an equitable remedy designed to strip the economic incentive for the violation. In the Mango Markets case, disgorgement is expected to be in the tens of millions.

**Restitution.** Payment to victims who suffered losses as a result of the violation. Restitution is ordered where identifiable victims suffered quantifiable harm.

**Injunctions.** Federal courts can permanently enjoin respondents from violating the CEA — essentially a court order saying "don't do this again." Violations of an injunction are contempt of court, carrying additional sanctions including imprisonment.

**Trading bans.** The CFTC can ban individuals from trading in any CFTC-regulated market and from registering with the CFTC. For career derivatives professionals, a permanent trading ban is effectively a professional death sentence.

**Registration revocation.** For registered entities (SDs, FCMs, CPOs, CTAs), the CFTC can revoke, suspend, or condition registration.

## Criminal Referrals

The CFTC itself does not prosecute criminal cases — it refers them to the **Department of Justice (DOJ)**. The CFTC's Division of Enforcement works closely with DOJ's Criminal Division and the relevant U.S. Attorney's Office to develop parallel cases.

Criminal penalties for CEA violations:

| Violation | Statute | Maximum Imprisonment | Maximum Fine |
|-----------|---------|---------------------|-------------|
| Manipulation | CEA §9(a)(2) | 10 years | $1,000,000 |
| Commodities fraud | 18 U.S.C. §1348 | 25 years | $250,000 (or §3571: greater of $250K or 2x gain/loss) |
| Spoofing | CEA §4c(a)(5)(C) | 10 years | $1,000,000 |
| Wash trading | CEA §4c(a) | 10 years | $1,000,000 |
| False reporting | CEA §9(a)(2) | 10 years | $1,000,000 |

Criminal prosecution is reserved for the most serious violations: manipulation, fraud, and willful violations of the CEA. The DOJ must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt — a significantly higher standard than the CFTC's civil preponderance standard. But the consequences are correspondingly severe: actual imprisonment, not just financial penalties.

## The Whistleblower Program

CFTC Rule Part 165 establishes a **whistleblower program** modeled on the SEC's successful version. Individuals who provide original information leading to a successful enforcement action with sanctions exceeding $1 million may receive an award of **10-30%** of the total sanctions collected.

The whistleblower program is a powerful enforcement multiplier. It incentivizes insiders — employees, counterparties, competitors, and even anonymous participants — to report violations. For DeFi, the program creates a specific risk: anyone with knowledge of a protocol's regulatory violations (including pseudonymous community members, former contributors, or competitors) can report to the CFTC with a financial incentive to do so.

!!! warning ""
    **Whistleblower Reality:** The CFTC's whistleblower program has awarded over $350 million to date. Awards have reached $200 million for a single whistleblower. If your DeFi protocol is operating in violation of the CEA, every community member, every former contributor, and every competitor who knows about it has a financial incentive — potentially millions of dollars — to report you. Whistleblowers can remain anonymous throughout the process.


## Cooperation Credit

The CFTC's 2017 **Advisory on Cooperation** outlines how cooperation by subjects and targets of enforcement investigations is evaluated and credited. Meaningful cooperation can result in:

- Reduced civil monetary penalties
- Favorable settlement terms
- More limited charges (e.g., failing to register vs. fraud)
- Declination of enforcement action in the most favorable cases

The CFTC evaluates cooperation across four factors: (1) the value of the cooperation to the investigation, (2) the extent and timeliness of cooperation, (3) the culpability of the cooperating entity, and (4) other relevant factors.

**DeFi application:** Several DeFi enforcement actions have involved cooperative settlements. Opyn's $250,000 penalty and ZeroEx's $200,000 penalty — both relatively modest for CEA violations — likely reflect significant cooperation. By contrast, Ooki DAO's refusal to participate in the proceedings resulted in a default judgment with no cooperation credit.

!!! info "AI Practitioner Note: Predicting Enforcement Severity"
    When assessing potential enforcement risk, prompt your AI model with:

    *"Analyze [description of potential CEA violation] and predict likely enforcement severity based on historical CFTC actions. Consider: (1) type of violation (registration, manipulation, fraud, recordkeeping), (2) comparable cases and their outcomes, (3) aggravating factors (customer harm, recidivism, obstruction), (4) mitigating factors (cooperation, remediation, good compliance program), (5) the CFTC's current enforcement priorities. Estimate the range of likely penalties (CMPs, disgorgement) and non-monetary remedies (injunctions, bans)."*

    **What to Verify:** AI models may not have current penalty amounts (which are inflation-adjusted annually) or recent enforcement actions (knowledge cutoff issues). Always verify penalty ranges against the CFTC's published annual enforcement results.

## Section Summary

- The CFTC enforces through administrative proceedings (registered entities) and federal court actions (unregistered entities, fraud)
- Penalties include CMPs (up to $190K per violation), disgorgement, restitution, injunctions, and trading bans
- Criminal referrals to DOJ carry maximum sentences of 10-25 years imprisonment
- The whistleblower program (10-30% of sanctions over $1M) creates powerful incentives for insiders to report violations
- Cooperation credit significantly reduces penalties — resistance (like Ooki DAO) results in the harshest outcomes
