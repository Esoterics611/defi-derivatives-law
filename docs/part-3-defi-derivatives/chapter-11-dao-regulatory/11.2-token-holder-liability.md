# 11.2 Token Holder Liability for Derivatives Violations

!!! info "Section Objectives"
    After completing this section, you will be able to:

    - Identify the specific conduct that creates liability for DAO token holders
    - Distinguish between governance participation levels and their associated legal risk
    - Assess the chilling effect of the Ooki DAO precedent on DAO governance
    - Apply liability analysis to hypothetical governance scenarios

---

## The Ooki DAO Holding

The court in *CFTC v. Ooki DAO* (No. 3:22-cv-05416, N.D. Cal.) did not merely hold that the DAO as an entity was liable. It held that **token holders who voted on governance proposals directing the DAO's operations were personally liable** as members of the unincorporated association. The CFTC's complaint specifically alleged that Ooki DAO token holders voted to:

- Continue operating the bZx/Ooki protocol as a leveraged derivatives trading platform
- Deploy protocol upgrades that maintained the unregistered trading functionality
- Use DAO treasury funds to incentivize liquidity provision for the platform

Each of these votes, in the CFTC's theory, constituted an act of **directing the association's illegal activity**. Token holders were not mere investors — they were operators.

## The Participation Spectrum

The critical legal question is: **what level of participation creates liability?** The answer is not binary but a spectrum:

| Participation Level | Specific Conduct | Liability Risk | Rationale |
|-------------------|-----------------|----------------|-----------|
| **Core contributors / founders** | Write code, deploy contracts, manage operations, often hold large token positions | **Very High** | Direct operators of the protocol; personally identifiable; Bean & Kistner were individually charged |
| **Active delegates** | Vote on behalf of token holders who have delegated voting power; submit proposals; participate in governance forums | **High** | Exercising governance authority over the association's operations; analogous to officers or directors |
| **Direct voters** | Cast governance votes on protocol proposals | **High** | The specific conduct identified in *Ooki DAO* as creating association membership and liability |
| **Proposal authors** | Draft and submit governance proposals (even without large token holdings) | **High** | Initiating governance actions that direct the association's conduct |
| **Forum participants** | Discuss proposals in governance forums without voting | **Low-Medium** | Participation in deliberation may evidence "common purpose" but falls short of directing conduct |
| **Passive holders** | Hold governance tokens without ever voting, delegating, or participating | **Low** | Strongest argument against liability — no active consent to or direction of the association's activities |
| **Former holders** | Sold governance tokens before enforcement action | **Uncertain** | Were they members during the violation period? Statute of limitations issues |

## The Chilling Effect

The Ooki DAO precedent has created a measurable chilling effect on DAO governance participation. If voting on governance proposals can create personal liability for the DAO's regulatory violations, rational token holders face a perverse incentive: **do not participate in governance**. This creates several problems:

**Governance apathy amplifies.** DeFi governance already suffers from low participation rates (typically 5-15% of circulating tokens vote on any given proposal). Adding personal liability risk makes the apathy problem worse — exactly the opposite of what DAO proponents intended.

**Informed participants withdraw.** The token holders most likely to understand the regulatory implications of the Ooki DAO precedent are also the most informed and engaged governance participants. If they withdraw from governance, the remaining voters are less sophisticated and less likely to identify regulatory risks in proposals.

**Power concentrates.** When small holders stop voting, governance power concentrates in large holders (whales), founding teams, and professional delegates — many of whom are already identifiable and accepting of higher risk profiles. This paradoxically makes the DAO more centralized, not less.

!!! danger "What Would You Advise?"
    **Scenario:** You hold 50,000 governance tokens in a DeFi perpetual futures protocol. A proposal comes to vote: "Deploy v3 upgrade adding 10x leverage on SOL/USD perpetual futures." You believe the upgrade will increase protocol revenue but know that offering leveraged derivatives to U.S. persons without CFTC registration violates the CEA.

    If you **vote yes**, you are directing the association to continue operating in violation of the CEA — personal liability under the *Ooki DAO* theory.

    If you **vote no**, you are participating in governance (acknowledging the association's existence) but opposing the illegal activity — likely a defense, but untested.

    If you **abstain**, you are not directing the activity but may still be considered a member of the association if you have voted on prior proposals.

    If you **sell your tokens before the vote**, you eliminate future governance exposure but may face liability for past participation.

    **There is no risk-free option for a token holder who has ever participated in governance of a non-compliant protocol.**

## Defenses and Mitigation

Token holders facing potential liability have several arguments, none of which have been tested in court:

**"I voted against the proposal."** This is likely the strongest defense. Voting against an illegal action demonstrates opposition to the association's conduct, analogous to a dissenting director in corporate law who is typically shielded from liability for board decisions they opposed.

**"I didn't know the protocol was non-compliant."** Ignorance of the law is generally not a defense to regulatory violations, but it may be relevant to intent-based penalties and the scope of remedies.

**"I delegated my tokens and didn't control how they were voted."** This creates a novel question: if you delegate governance power to a third party, are you liable for how they vote? Under agency law principles, a principal can be liable for an agent's actions within the scope of authority. Delegating governance tokens may create an agency relationship.

**"My holding was too small to influence the outcome."** This argument is analogous to claiming that a single vote in an election didn't matter. Under joint and several liability for unincorporated associations, each member is liable for the association's acts regardless of their individual influence.

---

## Section Summary

- The *Ooki DAO* court held that governance voting creates personal liability for association members
- Liability exists on a spectrum from core contributors (highest) to passive holders (lowest)
- The precedent creates a chilling effect: rational token holders are incentivized to avoid governance participation
- Voting against an illegal proposal is likely the strongest defense, but no defense has been tested
- There is no risk-free position for token holders who have participated in governance of non-compliant protocols
