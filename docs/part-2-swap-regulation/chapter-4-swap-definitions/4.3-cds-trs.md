# 4.3 Credit Default Swaps & Total Return Swaps

!!! info "Learning Objectives"
    After completing this section, you will be able to:
    - Explain CDS mechanics including credit events, settlement, and the protection buyer/seller dynamic
    - Distinguish CDS from insurance and articulate the legal significance of this distinction
    - Describe how total return swaps create synthetic ownership and hidden leverage
    - Map CDS and TRS concepts to DeFi credit protocols and leveraged position structures


!!! warning "Key Terms"
    - **Credit Event**: A contractually defined occurrence (default, bankruptcy, restructuring) that triggers CDS payment
    - **Reference Entity**: The company, sovereign, or obligation whose credit risk is transferred via CDS
    - **Protection Buyer**: The CDS counterparty that pays premiums and receives payment upon credit event (analogous to buying insurance)
    - **Protection Seller**: The CDS counterparty that collects premiums and pays upon credit event (analogous to writing insurance)
    - **Total Return Swap (TRS)**: A swap in which one party pays the total return (appreciation + income) of a reference asset, while the other pays a financing rate


---

## Credit Default Swaps: Mechanics

A credit default swap is a bilateral agreement in which one party (the protection buyer) makes periodic payments to another party (the protection seller) in exchange for compensation if a specified credit event occurs with respect to a reference entity.

### The Basic Structure

**Protection Buyer →** pays quarterly premium (e.g., 150 basis points per annum on $10M notional = $37,500/quarter)

**Protection Seller →** pays the loss amount if a credit event occurs (e.g., if reference entity defaults and recovery is 40%, seller pays $6 million on $10M notional)

### Credit Events

The 2014 ISDA Credit Derivatives Definitions (the standard documentation) define several credit events:

| Credit Event | Definition | Typical Trigger |
|-------------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Bankruptcy** | Reference entity files for bankruptcy or is declared insolvent | Chapter 11 filing |
| **Failure to Pay** | Reference entity fails to make scheduled payment after grace period | Missed bond coupon |
| **Restructuring** | Debt terms are changed to the detriment of creditors | Maturity extension, coupon reduction |
| **Obligation Acceleration** | Debt becomes due and payable before maturity due to default | Cross-default clause triggered |
| **Repudiation/Moratorium** | Sovereign entity refuses to honor obligations | Government declares debt moratorium |

### Settlement Methods

**Physical settlement:** Protection buyer delivers the defaulted obligation to the seller and receives par value. This was the original method but created "squeeze" problems when more CDS existed than deliverable bonds.

**Cash settlement:** Determined via ISDA Credit Event Auction. The auction establishes a final price for the defaulted obligations. Protection seller pays (100% - auction price) × notional. This is now the dominant method.

!!! success "Worked Example — Cash-Settled CDS"
    - Notional: $10 million
    - Reference entity: Company X
    - Credit event: Bankruptcy filing
    - ISDA auction final price: 35 cents on the dollar (recovery rate = 35%)
    - Protection seller pays: (100% - 35%) × $10M = **$6.5 million**


### The Insurance Distinction

CDS are economically similar to insurance, but legally they are **not** insurance. This distinction has enormous regulatory consequences:

| Feature | Insurance | CDS |
|---------|-----------|-----|
| **Insurable interest required** | Yes — must own the asset or face the loss | No — "naked" CDS permitted |
| **Regulated by** | State insurance commissioners | CFTC (broad-based) or SEC (single-name) |
| **Capital requirements** | Insurance reserves | Swap dealer capital rules |
| **Claim payment trigger** | Actual loss experienced | Credit event on reference entity |
| **Multiple policies on same risk** | Prohibited (indemnity principle) | Unlimited (notional can exceed outstanding debt) |

!!! danger "Why This Matters for DeFi"
    DeFi credit protocols that allow users to buy "protection" against protocol defaults (e.g., Nexus Mutual for smart contract failures, credit default products on real-world lending pools) sit squarely in this regulatory gray zone. If the product requires an insurable interest and is structured as insurance, it faces state insurance regulation. If it allows naked positions and is structured as a swap, it faces CFTC or SEC regulation. **Either way, it is regulated.** The question is which regime applies.


### Jurisdictional Classification

This is a critical classification point:

| CDS Type | Regulator | Basis |
|----------|-----------|-------|
| Single-name CDS (protection on one company) | **SEC** | Security-based swap |
| Narrow-based index CDS (≤9 reference entities) | **SEC** | Security-based swap |
| Broad-based index CDS (CDX, iTraxx) | **CFTC** | Swap |
| Sovereign CDS | **CFTC** | Swap |

## Total Return Swaps: Synthetic Ownership

A total return swap (TRS) is an agreement in which one party (the "total return receiver") receives the total economic return of a reference asset — price appreciation plus any income (dividends, coupons) — and pays a financing rate (e.g., SOFR + spread) to the other party (the "total return payer").

### The Mechanics

The TRS receiver has the economic exposure of owning the asset without actually buying it. The TRS payer holds the asset on its balance sheet but has hedged away its economic exposure.

| Cash Flow | Total Return Receiver | Total Return Payer |
|-----------|-----------------------|--------------------|
| Asset appreciates | Receives appreciation | Pays appreciation |
| Asset depreciates | Pays depreciation | Receives depreciation |
| Dividends/coupons | Receives them | Pays them through |
| Financing cost | Pays SOFR + spread | Receives SOFR + spread |

### Why TRS Enable Hidden Leverage

Total return swaps are the derivatives world's most powerful tool for **synthetic leverage and position concealment**:

**Leverage:** The TRS receiver posts margin (typically 10-20% of notional) to gain 100% economic exposure. A $100 million TRS with 15% margin gives $100 million exposure for $15 million in capital — 6.7x leverage.

**Concealment:** Under pre-2024 rules, TRS positions were not reported as beneficial ownership. A fund could build a massive economic position in a company's stock entirely via TRS without triggering 13D filing requirements. This is exactly what Archegos Capital did (see Case Study at end of chapter).

**Balance sheet efficiency:** The TRS receiver never holds the asset, keeping it off their balance sheet. The TRS payer holds the asset but reports a hedged position with minimal net exposure.

!!! warning "DeFi Parallel: Leveraged Positions via Protocols"
    DeFi perpetual futures protocols (GMX, dYdX, Hyperliquid) offer economically similar structures to TRS. A trader deposits collateral (margin), takes leveraged long/short exposure to an asset's total return (including funding rate payments that mirror the TRS financing leg), and can unwind the position at any time. The economic profile is nearly identical — the technology is different.

    Yield-bearing DeFi positions where a user deposits collateral and receives the total return of an underlying strategy (e.g., Yearn vaults, Aura Finance) also exhibit TRS-like characteristics.

## Section Summary

- CDS transfer credit risk from protection buyer to seller in exchange for periodic premiums
- CDS are legally not insurance — no insurable interest required, enabling naked CDS positions
- Single-name CDS are security-based swaps (SEC); broad-based index CDS are swaps (CFTC)
- Total return swaps provide synthetic ownership and leverage without holding the asset
- TRS enable position concealment and excessive leverage — the Archegos lesson
- DeFi credit protocols and leveraged perpetuals mirror CDS and TRS economics
