# 7.3 EMIR, MiFID II & Global Harmonization

!!! info "Section Objectives"
    After completing this section, you will be able to:

    - Describe EMIR's clearing, reporting, and risk mitigation requirements for OTC derivatives
    - Explain MiFID II/MiFIR's trading venue and transparency regime
    - Compare U.S. and EU approaches to derivatives regulation
    - Identify the key global regulatory bodies working toward harmonization

---

## EMIR: The EU's Dodd-Frank

The European Market Infrastructure Regulation (Regulation (EU) No 648/2012), adopted in 2012 and subsequently amended by EMIR REFIT (2019) and EMIR 3.0 (2024), is the EU's comprehensive framework for OTC derivatives. Like Dodd-Frank Title VII, EMIR was a post-2008 reform targeting the same systemic risks: uncleared bilateral exposures, opaque OTC markets, and inadequate counterparty risk management.

EMIR's three pillars parallel Dodd-Frank's:

| Pillar | EMIR Requirement | U.S. Equivalent | Key Differences |
|--------|-----------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Clearing** | Mandatory clearing for standardized OTC derivatives through authorized CCPs | CFTC clearing mandate through registered DCOs | EMIR uses ESMA (European Securities and Markets Authority) clearing determinations; U.S. uses CFTC Part 50. EMIR's clearing obligation covers a similar product set (IRS, index CDS) |
| **Reporting** | All derivatives (not just OTC) must be reported to registered Trade Repositories (TRs) | Part 43 (public) and Part 45 (regulatory) reporting to SDRs | EMIR requires reporting of **all** derivatives including exchange-traded; CFTC Part 45 covers only swaps. EMIR REFIT shifted reporting responsibility to the clearing counterparty |
| **Risk mitigation for uncleared derivatives** | Bilateral margin (IM and VM), portfolio reconciliation, dispute resolution, portfolio compression, timely confirmation | CFTC margin rules (Part 23), bilateral risk management requirements | EMIR's margin rules are largely harmonized with the U.S. under the BCBS/IOSCO framework, but implementation timelines and calculation methodologies differ |

## MiFID II / MiFIR: Trading Venue Regulation

While EMIR covers post-trade (clearing, reporting, risk mitigation), MiFID II (Directive 2014/65/EU) and MiFIR (Regulation (EU) No 600/2014) cover **pre-trade and trade execution**: where and how derivatives are traded, transparency requirements, and investor protection.

MiFID II's trading venue categories:

**Regulated Markets (RMs):** Traditional exchanges. Most relevant for listed derivatives (futures, listed options).

**Multilateral Trading Facilities (MTFs):** Multi-party trading venues that bring together multiple buying and selling interests. The EU analog to SEFs.

**Organised Trading Facilities (OTFs):** A new MiFID II category for non-equity instruments (including derivatives) where the operator exercises discretion in execution. No direct U.S. equivalent — OTFs allow a degree of dealer discretion that would be unusual for a SEF.

MiFIR imposes a **trading obligation** for derivatives that are subject to the clearing obligation and sufficiently liquid — equivalent to the CFTC's MAT determination. These must be executed on RMs, MTFs, or OTFs.

## Key U.S.-EU Divergences

Despite common G20 origins, the U.S. and EU frameworks diverge in important ways:

**Regulatory architecture:** The U.S. splits jurisdiction between the CFTC (swaps, commodity futures) and SEC (security-based swaps). The EU uses ESMA as a centralized derivatives regulator (with national competent authorities implementing at the member state level). This means the "is it a swap or a security-based swap?" question that consumes so much U.S. regulatory analysis does not exist in the EU framework.

**Scope of reporting:** EMIR requires reporting of all derivatives; the U.S. system splits between CFTC reporting (swaps) and SEC reporting (security-based swaps), with exchange-traded derivatives reported through different channels.

**Extraterritoriality approach:** The CFTC's cross-border framework is more aggressively extraterritorial than EMIR. EMIR applies primarily to EU-established entities and their transactions, with less extensive reach to non-EU counterparties than the CFTC's "U.S. person" framework.

**Equivalence vs. substituted compliance:** The EU's equivalence framework (under EMIR Article 13) is structurally similar to CFTC substituted compliance but has been applied more selectively and has become politically contentious (particularly regarding UK CCPs post-Brexit).

## Beyond the U.S. and EU

Several other jurisdictions have implemented G20 derivatives reforms:

**United Kingdom:** Post-Brexit, the UK onshored EMIR and MiFID II as UK EMIR and UK MiFIR, with the FCA and Bank of England as regulators. The UK is diverging from the EU framework in certain areas, creating a third regulatory regime for firms that previously complied with a single EU rulebook.

**Singapore (MAS):** The Securities and Futures Act provides a comprehensive derivatives framework. Singapore has been proactive in regulating digital asset derivatives, with MAS licensing requirements for crypto derivative platforms.

**Hong Kong (SFC/HKMA):** The Securities and Futures Ordinance covers OTC derivatives. The SFC has taken a relatively permissive approach to crypto derivatives compared to the CFTC, licensing platforms like HashKey and OSL.

**Japan (JFSA):** The Financial Instruments and Exchange Act covers derivatives. Japan has been relatively advanced in crypto asset regulation, with a clear licensing framework for crypto exchanges since 2017.

## Global Harmonization Efforts

The Financial Stability Board (FSB), IOSCO (International Organization of Securities Commissions), and CPMI (Committee on Payments and Market Infrastructures) coordinate global derivatives reform. Key harmonization achievements include the BCBS/IOSCO margin framework for uncleared derivatives (adopted globally), the LEI system (global identifier), and the UPI standard (harmonized product identification). Key failures include trade execution requirements (wildly different approaches across jurisdictions), cross-border recognition (politically contentious and incomplete), and digital asset classification (no global consensus).

---

## Section Summary

- EMIR is the EU's Dodd-Frank equivalent, with parallel clearing, reporting, and risk mitigation pillars
- MiFID II/MiFIR covers trading venues and transparency, with MTFs and OTFs as the EU's SEF equivalents
- Key U.S.-EU divergences include regulatory architecture, scope of reporting, extraterritoriality, and equivalence mechanisms
- The UK, Singapore, Hong Kong, and Japan have their own frameworks, creating a fragmented global landscape
- Harmonization has succeeded for margin and identifiers but failed for trade execution and digital asset classification
