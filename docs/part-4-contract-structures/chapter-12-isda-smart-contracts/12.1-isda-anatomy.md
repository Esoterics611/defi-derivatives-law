# 12.1 Anatomy of the ISDA Master Agreement

!!! info "Section Objectives"
    After completing this section, you will be able to:

    - Describe the three-layer ISDA documentation architecture
    - Identify the key sections of the 2002 ISDA Master Agreement
    - Explain the single agreement concept and why it enables netting
    - Map each ISDA structural element to a DeFi smart contract equivalent

---

## The Three-Layer Architecture

ISDA documentation follows a **three-layer structure** that separates standardized terms from customized terms from transaction-specific terms:

**Layer 1 — The Master Agreement.** The pre-printed standard form published by ISDA. Two versions are in widespread use: the 1992 ISDA Master Agreement and the 2002 ISDA Master Agreement (the latter is now standard for most new relationships). The Master Agreement contains the boilerplate provisions that govern the entire derivatives relationship: representations, covenants, events of default, termination events, netting, and close-out procedures. It is virtually never modified directly — instead, customization occurs through the Schedule.

**Layer 2 — The Schedule.** A bespoke document negotiated between the two counterparties that modifies, supplements, and amends the Master Agreement's standard terms. The Schedule is where the real negotiation happens: which events of default apply, what threshold amounts trigger cross-default, which governing law controls, what additional representations are required. Every bilateral derivatives relationship has a unique Schedule.

**Layer 3 — Confirmations.** Transaction-specific documents that record the economic terms of each individual derivative trade: notional amount, tenor, fixed rate, floating rate benchmark, payment dates, calculation methodology. Each new trade under the relationship generates a new Confirmation. Confirmations incorporate ISDA Definitions booklets (standardized templates for specific product types) by reference.

Together, these three layers form a **single agreement** — a concept that is foundational to how ISDA documentation works.

## The Single Agreement Concept

Section 1(c) of the 2002 Master Agreement states:

The Master Agreement, the Schedule, each Confirmation, and the Credit Support Annex together constitute a **single agreement** between the parties. This principle is not merely organizational — it has critical legal consequences:

**Netting.** Because all transactions are part of a single agreement, obligations can be netted against each other. If Party A owes Party B $10 million on one swap and Party B owes Party A $7 million on another, only the net $3 million changes hands. Without the single agreement concept, each swap would be a separate obligation, and netting would not be legally enforceable in many jurisdictions.

**Close-out.** If an event of default occurs, the non-defaulting party can terminate all transactions simultaneously and calculate a single net termination amount. This prevents "cherry-picking" — the defaulting party (or its bankruptcy trustee) cannot selectively enforce profitable transactions while repudiating unprofitable ones.

**Insolvency protection.** In many jurisdictions, the single agreement concept provides a legal basis for enforcing netting in insolvency. Bankruptcy safe harbor provisions (discussed in Chapter 14) protect the close-out netting right precisely because the transactions are part of a single agreement.

## Key Sections of the 2002 Master Agreement

| Section | Title | Purpose | DeFi Smart Contract Equivalent |
|---------|-------|---------|-------------------------------|
| **1** | Interpretation | Defines terms, establishes single agreement concept | Smart contract constructor, interface definitions |
| **2** | Obligations | Payment and delivery obligations, netting of payments | Transfer functions, payment settlement logic |
| **3** | Representations | Each party's representations about its legal status, authority, and compliance | No direct equivalent — smart contracts don't verify legal status of counterparties |
| **4** | Agreements | Ongoing covenants (furnish information, maintain authorizations, comply with laws) | No direct equivalent — code does not impose ongoing behavioral obligations |
| **5** | Events of Default & Termination Events | Triggers for early termination (failure to pay, breach, bankruptcy, cross-default) | Liquidation triggers (LTV threshold breach, oracle price deviation) |
| **6** | Early Termination | Close-out mechanics, calculation of termination payments | Liquidation execution, position unwinding, collateral return |
| **7** | Transfer | Assignment and transfer restrictions | Token transfer restrictions (if any), position NFTs |
| **8** | Contractual Currency | All payments in agreed currency, conversion mechanics | Denomination token (USDC, ETH), no currency conversion issues |
| **9** | Miscellaneous | Notices, governing law, amendments, waiver | Governance parameters, upgrade mechanisms |
| **10** | Offices | Multi-branch provisions | Not applicable — smart contracts have no physical offices |
| **11** | Expenses | Enforcement costs, stamp tax | Gas costs (borne by transaction initiator) |
| **12** | Notices | Delivery requirements for legal notices | Event emissions, on-chain notifications |
| **13** | Governing Law | Choice of law and jurisdiction | Code as law (no governing law clause); potential legal wrapper jurisdiction |
| **14** | Definitions | Defined terms | Solidity type definitions, constant declarations |

## 1992 vs. 2002: Key Differences

The 2002 Master Agreement introduced several improvements over the 1992 version:

**Close-out valuation (Section 6).** The 1992 version offered two valuation methods: "Market Quotation" (polling dealers for replacement cost) and "Loss" (reasonable determination by the non-defaulting party). The 2002 version replaced both with a single "Close-out Amount" standard — the amount that would be paid or received to replace the terminated transactions, determined using commercially reasonable procedures. This is more flexible and avoids the problem of illiquid markets where dealer quotations are unavailable.

**Force Majeure.** The 2002 version added a force majeure termination event (Section 5(b)(ii)) — allowing termination when external events beyond either party's control prevent performance. The 1992 version had no force majeure provision. In DeFi terms, this would cover events like blockchain network outages or oracle failures — but smart contracts have no mechanism to invoke force majeure.

**Set-off rights (Section 6(f)).** The 2002 version includes a broader set-off right, allowing the non-defaulting party to set off termination amounts against other obligations between the parties (including obligations under separate agreements). This strengthens the netting position.

!!! warning "What ISDA Handles That Smart Contracts Don't"
    The most important gaps between ISDA documentation and DeFi smart contracts are not economic — they are **judgmental**. ISDA contracts rely on human discretion at critical moments: the Calculation Agent uses "commercially reasonable procedures" to determine close-out amounts; parties exercise judgment in declaring events of default; force majeure requires assessment of whether an event is beyond a party's control. Smart contracts have no mechanism for subjective judgment. When a DeFi protocol liquidates a position, there is no "commercially reasonable" assessment — there is an oracle price and a threshold, and the code executes. This mechanical certainty is both DeFi's strength (no disputes about whether a liquidation was fair) and its weakness (no ability to exercise forbearance when circumstances warrant it).

## Section Summary

- ISDA documentation uses a three-layer architecture: Master Agreement (standard terms), Schedule (custom terms), Confirmations (trade terms)
- The single agreement concept enables netting and close-out across all transactions
- The 2002 Master Agreement is the current standard, improving on 1992 with unified close-out valuation and force majeure
- Smart contracts implement ISDA's economic mechanics but lack the subjective judgment provisions that handle edge cases
