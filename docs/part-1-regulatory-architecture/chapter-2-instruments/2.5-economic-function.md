# 2.5 The Economic Function of Each Instrument

Understanding WHY each derivative instrument exists — what economic problem it solves — is essential for regulatory analysis. Regulators classify instruments based on their **economic substance**, not their label or technical implementation. A DeFi protocol that performs the economic function of a futures exchange will be regulated as a futures exchange, regardless of whether it calls itself a "decentralized trading protocol."

## 2.5.1 Economic Functions

| Instrument | Primary Economic Function | Who Uses It | Example |
|------------|--------------------------|-------------|---------|
| **Futures** | **Price discovery** and **standardized hedging** — establishing transparent benchmark prices and allowing standardized risk transfer | Commodity producers/consumers, speculators, institutional hedgers | Airline buys crude oil futures to lock in jet fuel costs |
| **Options** | **Asymmetric risk management** — protection against adverse price movements while preserving upside potential | Portfolio managers, insurers, strategic hedgers | Farmer buys put options on wheat to guarantee minimum sale price |
| **Forwards** | **Customized hedging** — tailoring delivery terms, quantities, and timing to specific commercial needs | Corporations with specific exposure profiles | Importer locks in exchange rate for a specific future payment |
| **Swaps** | **Cash flow transformation** — converting one type of financial exposure into another without changing the underlying position | Banks, corporations, asset managers | Company converts floating-rate debt to fixed-rate through an IRS |

## 2.5.2 The Risk Transfer Chain

Derivatives exist because different market participants have different risk preferences and different information. The derivatives market creates a mechanism for transferring risk from those who don't want it to those who are willing to bear it (for a price).

The basic chain:

**Hedger** (wants to reduce risk) → **Derivative Contract** → **Speculator** (willing to take risk for potential profit)

In traditional markets, this chain is intermediated by dealers, brokers, and exchanges. In DeFi, the chain is intermediated by smart contracts and liquidity pools. But the economic function is identical — risk is being transferred from one party to another through a derivative instrument.

## 2.5.3 The DeFi Instrument Map

Every major DeFi protocol type maps to a traditional derivative instrument:

| DeFi Protocol Type | Traditional Equivalent | Economic Function | Regulatory Classification |
|-------------------|----------------------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| **Perpetual futures** (dYdX, GMX, Hyperliquid) | Swaps (or futures, depending on structure) | Leveraged directional exposure with funding rate exchange | Swap or future — CFTC jurisdiction |
| **Options vaults** (Opyn, Lyra, Dopex) | OTC options / exchange-traded options | Asymmetric risk transfer, yield generation | Commodity options — CFTC jurisdiction |
| **Synthetic assets** (Synthetix, Mirror) | Total return swaps | Exposure to an asset's price without owning the asset | Swap — CFTC (or SEC if underlying is a security) |
| **Prediction markets** (Polymarket, Augur) | Binary options / event contracts | Binary outcome speculation | Event contracts — CFTC jurisdiction |
| **Yield tokenization** (Pendle) | Interest rate swaps | Separation and trading of yield vs. principal | Potentially swap — analysis required |
| **Structured vaults** (Ribbon, Friktion) | Structured notes / exotic options | Automated options strategies packaged as yield products | Option / structured product — likely CFTC |
| **LP positions** (Uniswap v3 concentrated) | Short straddle / short strangle (options) | Market making with convex payoff profile | Ambiguous — subject of active debate |

!!! warning ""
    **The LP-as-Derivative Debate**: Research by Lambert, Fromentin, and others has shown that concentrated Uniswap v3 LP positions have payoff profiles mathematically equivalent to selling options. If an LP position is economically equivalent to a short put option, is it a derivative for regulatory purposes? The CFTC has not yet addressed this question directly, but the functional equivalence argument (Chapter 8) could extend CFTC jurisdiction to AMM liquidity provision.


## 2.5.4 The Instrument Classification Framework

When analyzing any DeFi protocol, ask these questions to determine which traditional instrument it most closely resembles:

1. **Does the product involve leverage?** If yes → likely a future, swap, or leveraged retail commodity transaction
2. **Is there an expiration date?** If yes → future or option. If no → swap (perpetual)
3. **Does the holder have an obligation or only a right?** Right only → option. Obligation → future/swap/forward
4. **Is settlement in the underlying asset or in cash?** Underlying → potentially an exempt forward. Cash → derivative
5. **Are cash flows exchanged periodically?** If yes → swap (funding rate payments = periodic cash flow exchange)
6. **Is the payoff binary (all-or-nothing)?** If yes → binary option / event contract
7. **Does the product create synthetic exposure to an asset without owning it?** If yes → swap (total return swap equivalent)

    Instrument classification is a core AI task. Feed a DeFi protocol's documentation and smart contract description to an AI model and ask it to classify the protocol's products using this framework.

    **Prompt Template:**

    ```
    5. Are periodic cash flows exchanged (e.g., funding rates)?

    Product: [description]
    ```

    **What to Verify:** AI models tend to over-classify DeFi products as "novel" or "unique" rather than mapping them to existing regulatory categories. Push back on this — regulators will classify based on economic substance and function, not on technical novelty.
