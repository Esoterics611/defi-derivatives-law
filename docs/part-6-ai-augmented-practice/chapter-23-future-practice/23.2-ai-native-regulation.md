# 23.2 AI-Native Regulation and Machine-Readable Rules

!!! info "Section Objectives"
    After completing this section, you will be able to:

    - Explain the concept of machine-readable regulation and its current precedents
    - Describe how XBRL and regulatory reporting automation pave the way for programmatic compliance
    - Assess CFTC LabCFTC and other regulatory technology initiatives
    - Anticipate how AI-native regulation will change derivatives practice

---

## The Vision: Regulation as Code

Today, CFTC regulations exist as natural language text — thousands of pages of statutes, rules, guidance documents, and enforcement orders, written by lawyers for lawyers. AI can read this text, but it must interpret it — introducing ambiguity, hallucination risk, and the constant danger of misunderstanding nuance.

The future: regulations encoded as structured data — machine-readable formats that AI systems can process directly, without interpretation. Not "AI reads the rule and tells you what it means" but "AI queries the rule engine and gets a definitive compliance answer."

This is not science fiction. The building blocks already exist.

## Current Precedents

### XBRL and Financial Reporting

The closest existing precedent is XBRL (eXtensible Business Reporting Language). Since 2009, the SEC has required public companies to file financial reports in XBRL format — structured data tags that make every financial figure machine-readable. What used to require human analysts reading 10-K filings is now queryable in seconds.

XBRL proves the concept: regulatory requirements can be encoded as structured data, and regulated entities can report compliance in machine-readable formats. The question is when — not whether — this model extends to derivatives regulation.

### LEI and UPI Systems

The Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) system and the Unique Product Identifier (UPI) system already provide machine-readable classification for swap counterparties and instruments. Every swap report submitted to a swap data repository includes an LEI for each counterparty and a UPI for the product — enabling automated matching, aggregation, and surveillance.

### ISDA Common Domain Model (CDM)

ISDA's Common Domain Model (see Section 12.5) represents derivatives transactions as standardized digital objects. The CDM encodes trade lifecycle events — execution, confirmation, clearing, settlement — as structured data that both humans and machines can process. The CDM is, in effect, machine-readable derivatives documentation.

## From Readable to Queryable: The Regulatory API

The logical endpoint of machine-readable regulation is a **Regulatory API** — a programmatic interface where compliance questions are answered by querying a rules engine rather than reading regulatory text:

| Regulatory Process | Current (Human-Readable) | Future (Machine-Readable) | Impact on Practice |
|-------------------|-------------------------|--------------------------|-------------------|
| **Swap classification** | Read CEA § 1a(47), apply definition to facts, form legal judgment | Query API with instrument parameters, receive classification with confidence score | Instant classification replaces hours of analysis; human review for edge cases only |
| **Registration determination** | Analyze entity activities against SD/MSP definitions across multiple rules | Submit entity profile to API, receive registration requirement determination | Routine determinations automated; complex multi-entity structures still require judgment |
| **Position limit checking** | Look up applicable limits in 17 CFR Part 150, calculate net position across accounts | Real-time API query checks position against current limits | Continuous compliance replaces periodic review |
| **Reporting requirements** | Determine which fields are required under Part 43/45 rules for each trade type | API returns required reporting template with field specifications for given trade parameters | Zero ambiguity on reporting requirements; automated report generation |
| **Cross-border applicability** | Analyze CFTC cross-border guidance to determine which rules apply to which legs | API determines applicable regulatory regime based on counterparty jurisdiction, booking entity, and execution venue | Automated jurisdictional determination for routine trades |

## The Regulator's AI

The compliance equation has two sides. While practitioners deploy AI to ensure compliance, regulators are deploying AI to detect violations. This creates an arms race dynamic that practitioners must understand.

### CFTC Technology Initiatives

**LabCFTC**: Established in 2017, LabCFTC serves as the CFTC's fintech innovation hub. It provides a point of contact for fintech companies, has published primers on blockchain and AI, and facilitates dialogue between innovators and regulators. LabCFTC has explicitly explored how AI can enhance market surveillance.

**Market surveillance**: The CFTC already uses algorithmic surveillance tools to monitor futures and swaps markets for manipulation, spoofing, and position limit violations. AI enhancements to these tools would enable pattern detection across larger datasets and identification of novel manipulation strategies.

**Enforcement prioritization**: AI tools can help the CFTC's Division of Enforcement triage incoming tips, complaints, and referrals — prioritizing cases with the highest potential impact and strongest evidence.

!!! warning "The Asymmetry Warning"
    If regulators deploy AI to detect violations and practitioners do not deploy AI to ensure compliance, an untenable asymmetry emerges. The regulator's AI will detect patterns and anomalies that human-only compliance programs miss. Practitioners who rely solely on manual compliance processes will face increasingly sophisticated regulatory scrutiny without comparable analytical tools.

    The implication is clear: **AI-augmented compliance is not optional — it is a competitive and regulatory necessity.**

## What This Means for Practice

### Short-Term (1-3 years)

- **Regulatory reporting automation matures** — SDR reporting becomes increasingly automated; human intervention limited to exceptions
- **RegTech vendors proliferate** — Specialized tools for CFTC compliance gain traction; practitioners evaluate and integrate third-party AI compliance solutions
- **CFTC issues technology guidance** — Expect CFTC advisory guidance or proposed rules addressing the use of AI in compliance, potentially including standards for AI-generated regulatory filings

### Medium-Term (3-7 years)

- **Machine-readable rules pilot programs** — The CFTC or a global standard-setter publishes a subset of rules in machine-readable format, likely starting with position limits or reporting requirements
- **Compliance APIs emerge** — Third-party services offer programmatic compliance checking against encoded regulatory requirements
- **Standardized AI compliance audit** — Regulators develop frameworks for auditing AI compliance systems, requiring transparency about how AI tools make compliance determinations

### Long-Term (7+ years)

- **Native digital regulation** — New rules are published simultaneously in natural language and machine-readable format, as a standard part of the rulemaking process
- **Automated enforcement detection** — Regulatory AI operates in real-time across market data, identifying potential violations as they occur rather than through post-hoc investigation
- **Compliance certification** — AI compliance systems undergo certification processes analogous to financial audits, providing third-party assurance that the system correctly implements applicable rules

## The Practitioner's Response

For derivatives practitioners reading this today, the immediate action items are:

1. **Build compliance data infrastructure** — Structure your compliance data in machine-readable formats now, even if the regulatory API doesn't exist yet. When it does, you'll be ready
2. **Track LabCFTC and CFTC technology initiatives** — Monitor the CFTC's technology advisory committee and LabCFTC publications for signals about where machine-readable regulation is heading
3. **Invest in AI compliance tools** — The practitioners who deploy AI compliance systems now will have years of training data, refined prompts, and operational experience when AI compliance becomes the regulatory expectation
4. **Engage in the regulatory process** — File comment letters on technology-related proposed rules. The regulatory framework for AI compliance is being written now, and practitioner input shapes it
