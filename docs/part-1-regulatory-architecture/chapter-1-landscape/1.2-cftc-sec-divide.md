# 1.2 CFTC vs. SEC: The Jurisdictional Divide

Understanding the boundary between CFTC and SEC jurisdiction is the single most important analytical skill in derivatives law. Get the classification wrong, and you're complying with the wrong regulator's rules — or worse, complying with no one's rules while both agencies sharpen their enforcement tools.

## 1.2.1 The Basic Division

The jurisdictional divide is deceptively simple in principle:

- **The CFTC** regulates **commodities**, **futures**, **options on futures**, and **swaps** (non-security-based).
- **The SEC** regulates **securities**, **security-based swaps**, and **options on securities**.

The complexity arises from two facts. First, the definition of "commodity" under the CEA is extraordinarily broad — it includes "all services, rights, and interests... in which contracts for future delivery are presently or in the future dealt in" (CEA §1a(9)). This definition is so expansive that the CFTC has successfully argued it covers everything from onions to Bitcoin. Second, many instruments have characteristics of both commodities and securities, creating jurisdictional gray zones that both agencies claim.

## 1.2.2 The CFTC: Jurisdiction and Powers

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission was established by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission Act of 1974, splitting off from the Department of Agriculture. Its jurisdiction covers:

| Domain | Statutory Basis | Examples |
|--------|----------------|----------|
| **Futures** | CEA §2(a)(1)(A) | CME corn futures, CBOE Bitcoin futures |
| **Options on Commodities** | CEA §4c(b) | Crude oil options, agricultural options |
| **Swaps** | CEA §1a(47), Dodd-Frank Title VII | Interest rate swaps, commodity swaps, CDS (non-security-based) |
| **Retail Commodity Transactions** | CEA §2(c)(2)(D) | Leveraged/margined retail crypto transactions |
| **Anti-Fraud/Manipulation** | CEA §6(c), §9(a)(2) | Fraud involving ANY commodity — including digital assets |

The CFTC's anti-fraud and anti-manipulation authority is particularly important for DeFi. Even when the CFTC does not have direct regulatory jurisdiction over a spot commodity market (it doesn't regulate spot Bitcoin trading), it has **enforcement authority** over fraud and manipulation in any market involving a commodity. This is the legal basis for almost every CFTC action against crypto platforms.

!!! warning ""
    **Critical Distinction**: The CFTC has *regulatory* jurisdiction over derivatives (futures, options, swaps) and *enforcement* jurisdiction over fraud and manipulation in commodity markets. These are different powers. The CFTC cannot write rules for Bitcoin spot markets, but it CAN bring fraud and manipulation cases in those markets.


## 1.2.3 The SEC: Jurisdiction and Powers

The Securities and Exchange Commission, established by the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, regulates:

| Domain | Statutory Basis | Examples |
|--------|----------------|----------|
| **Securities** | Securities Act §2(a)(1), Exchange Act §3(a)(10) | Stocks, bonds, investment contracts |
| **Security-Based Swaps** | Exchange Act §3(a)(68) | Single-name CDS, equity total return swaps |
| **Options on Securities** | Exchange Act §9(b) | Stock options, equity index options |
| **Investment Contracts** | Howey test (SEC v. W.J. Howey Co., 1946) | Token offerings that pass the Howey test |

The SEC's jurisdiction over crypto assets hinges largely on the **Howey test**: an instrument is a security if there is (1) an investment of money, (2) in a common enterprise, (3) with a reasonable expectation of profits, (4) derived from the efforts of others. The SEC has used this test to classify numerous token offerings as unregistered securities offerings.

## 1.2.4 The Jurisdictional Divide: Side-by-Side

| Dimension | CFTC | SEC |
|-----------|------|-----|
| **Primary statute** | Commodity Exchange Act (CEA) | Securities Act + Securities Exchange Act |
| **Core jurisdiction** | Futures, options, swaps on non-securities | Securities, security-based swaps |
| **Definitional anchor** | "Commodity" (CEA §1a(9)) — extremely broad | "Security" (Howey test) — facts-and-circumstances |
| **Registration regime** | Swap dealers, FCMs, CPOs, CTAs, DCMs, SEFs | Broker-dealers, exchanges, ATS, clearing agencies |
| **Self-regulatory org** | NFA (National Futures Association) | FINRA (Financial Industry Regulatory Authority) |
| **Enforcement style** | Aggressive on market manipulation, increasingly targeting DeFi | Aggressive on unregistered securities, ICO enforcement |
| **Penalty authority** | CMPs up to $190,132/violation; disgorgement; restitution | CMPs; disgorgement; injunctions; officer/director bars |
| **Criminal referrals** | To DOJ (commodities fraud under 18 USC §1348) | To DOJ (securities fraud under 15 USC §78ff) |
| **DeFi focus areas** | Unregistered derivatives platforms, perpetual futures, prediction markets | Unregistered token offerings, staking-as-security, lending platforms |
| **Digital asset position** | Bitcoin and Ether are commodities | Many altcoins are securities (contested) |

## 1.2.5 The Shad-Johnson Accord and Mixed Swaps

The jurisdictional divide between the CFTC and SEC was formalized in the **Shad-Johnson Accord** of 1982, an agreement between CFTC Chairman Philip McBride Johnson and SEC Chairman John S.R. Shad. The accord prohibited single-stock futures (giving the SEC effective veto power) while preserving CFTC jurisdiction over broad-based index futures.

Dodd-Frank complicated this by creating three categories of swaps:

| Category | Regulator | Definition | Example |
|----------|-----------|------------|---------|
| **Swap** | CFTC | Swap based on non-securities: commodities, interest rates, FX, broad-based indices | Interest rate swap, oil price swap, Bitcoin swap |
| **Security-Based Swap** | SEC | Swap based on a single security, a loan, or a narrow-based security index | Single-name CDS, equity total return swap on one stock |
| **Mixed Swap** | Both (joint CFTC/SEC) | Swap with characteristics of both a swap and a security-based swap | Swap referencing both an equity index and an interest rate |

!!! danger ""
    **The DeFi Classification Nightmare**: A DeFi protocol that offers perpetual futures on a token that the SEC considers a security is potentially creating a security-based swap — placing it under SEC jurisdiction, not just CFTC jurisdiction. If that same protocol also offers perpetual futures on Bitcoin (a commodity), it faces CFTC jurisdiction for that product. A single protocol can simultaneously trigger both agencies' enforcement authority for different products on the same platform.


## 1.2.6 Regulatory Classification Decision Tree

When you encounter any derivative instrument — traditional or DeFi — work through this classification logic:

**Step 1: Is this a derivative?** Does the instrument's value derive from an underlying reference asset, rate, index, or event? If no, it's probably a spot transaction. If yes, continue.

**Step 2: Is the underlying a security?** Apply the Howey test to the underlying asset. If the underlying is a single security, a loan, or a narrow-based security index → likely SEC jurisdiction (security-based swap). If the underlying is a commodity, interest rate, FX rate, or broad-based index → likely CFTC jurisdiction (swap or future).

**Step 3: Is the instrument standardized and exchange-traded?** If yes → futures or options (CFTC if commodity-based, SEC if security-based). If no → swap or security-based swap (OTC).

**Step 4: Who are the counterparties?** If retail (non-eligible contract participants) → additional restrictions apply. CFTC requires exchange trading for retail commodity transactions unless actual delivery occurs within 28 days.

**Step 5: Is this offered to U.S. persons?** If yes → U.S. regulatory jurisdiction applies regardless of where the protocol or platform is domiciled.

!!! success "AI Practitioner Note"
    This decision tree is exactly the kind of structured analysis that AI performs well — IF you set up the prompt correctly.

    **Prompt Template:**
    ```
    Act as a CFTC/SEC jurisdictional analyst. For the following DeFi protocol product, 
    walk through this classification:

    1. Is this a derivative? What is the underlying reference asset?
    2. Is the underlying a "commodity" under CEA §1a(9) or a "security" under Howey?
    3. Is this a swap (CEA §1a(47)), security-based swap (Exchange Act §3(a)(68)), 
       future, or option?
    4. Are counterparties Eligible Contract Participants under CEA §1a(18)?
    5. Is this offered to US persons?
    6. What registration requirements apply?

    Product: [describe the DeFi product]
    ```

    **AI Limitation Warning:** AI models frequently conflate CFTC regulatory jurisdiction (over derivatives) with CFTC enforcement jurisdiction (over fraud/manipulation in commodity markets). These are different legal authorities with different thresholds. Always verify which type of jurisdiction the AI is asserting.
