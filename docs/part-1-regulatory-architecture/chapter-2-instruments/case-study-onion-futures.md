# Case Study: The Onion Futures Act — When Congress Banned a Derivative

!!! info "Case Study Overview"
    The Onion Futures Act of 1958 is the only time in American history that Congress banned trading in a specific commodity's futures contracts. The story reveals how derivatives markets work, what happens when they're manipulated, and why the ban ultimately harmed the very farmers it was meant to protect. It's also a cautionary tale for DeFi regulators who might be tempted to ban rather than regulate.


## The Setup: Onions and the Chicago Mercantile Exchange

In the 1950s, onion futures were one of the most actively traded contracts on the Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME). Onion futures served a legitimate economic purpose: onion farmers could lock in prices before harvest, and onion distributors could secure supply at known costs. The contracts were standardized, exchange-traded, and cleared — a textbook derivatives market.

The problem was manipulation. In 1955, two traders — **Sam Siegel** and **Vincent Kosuga** — executed one of the most audacious commodity manipulations in history. They accumulated massive long positions in onion futures while simultaneously hoarding physical onions in Chicago warehouses. By controlling both the futures market and a significant portion of the physical supply, they drove onion prices to extraordinary highs.

Then they reversed course. Siegel and Kosuga began selling their futures positions short while dumping their physical onion stockpiles onto the market. Onion prices collapsed. At one point, a 50-pound bag of onions sold for less than the bag itself. Onion farmers were devastated.

## The Political Response

Outraged onion farmers lobbied Congress. Representative Gerald Ford (later President Ford) of Michigan — a major onion-producing state — championed legislation to ban onion futures trading entirely. The **Onion Futures Act** was signed into law on August 28, 1958, amending the CEA to prohibit "any contract for the delivery of onions" on any exchange regulated by the CEA.

The act is notable for what it reveals about the political economy of derivatives regulation: rather than strengthening market surveillance, increasing margin requirements, or prosecuting the manipulators under existing anti-manipulation provisions, Congress chose the blunt instrument of an outright ban.

## The Aftermath: Did the Ban Help?

Economists have studied onion price behavior before and after the ban extensively. The consensus finding is striking: **the ban made price volatility worse, not better**.

| Metric | Before Ban (with futures) | After Ban (no futures) |
|--------|--------------------------|----------------------|
| **Price volatility** | Lower | Higher |
| **Price discovery** | Transparent (exchange-traded) | Opaque (bilateral negotiation) |
| **Farmer hedging ability** | Available via futures | Eliminated |
| **Storage/inventory management** | Price signals guided storage decisions | No forward-looking price signals |

Without futures, onion farmers lost the ability to hedge their crop before harvest. Without exchange-traded price discovery, the market lost its most efficient mechanism for signaling future supply and demand conditions. The very farmers the ban was intended to protect were harmed by its unintended consequences.

## The Lesson for DeFi Regulation

The Onion Futures Act is the canonical example of why banning a derivative instrument — rather than regulating the market in which it trades — is usually counterproductive.

The manipulation that triggered the ban was not caused by the existence of onion futures. It was caused by the absence of adequate position limits, market surveillance, and anti-manipulation enforcement. Futures markets provide genuine economic value — price discovery, risk transfer, and hedging — that exists regardless of whether some participants abuse the market.

The parallel to DeFi is direct:

| 1950s Onion Market | 2020s DeFi Derivatives |
|--------------------|----------------------|
| Futures served real hedging and price discovery functions | DeFi derivatives serve real hedging and trading functions |
| Manipulation occurred due to inadequate surveillance | Manipulation occurs due to MEV, oracle attacks, and information asymmetry |
| Congress banned the instrument instead of regulating the market | Some regulators advocate banning DeFi derivatives instead of creating compliance frameworks |
| The ban increased volatility and harmed farmers | Banning DeFi derivatives would push activity offshore and reduce transparency |

!!! warning ""
    **The Regulatory Takeaway**: The CFTC's current approach to DeFi — enforcement actions that demand compliance, not bans that prohibit innovation — is more sophisticated than Congress's response to onion manipulation in 1958. But the Onion Futures Act remains a warning: heavy-handed regulation that eliminates derivative markets rather than improving them tends to produce worse outcomes for the participants it aims to protect.


The Onion Futures Act remains in effect today. Onions are the only commodity in the United States for which futures trading is prohibited. Every other commodity — including Bitcoin — can be traded on regulated exchanges, subject to the regulatory framework this book explains.
