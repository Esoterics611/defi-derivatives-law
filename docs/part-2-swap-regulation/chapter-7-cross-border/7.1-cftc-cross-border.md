# 7.1 The CFTC Cross-Border Framework

!!! info "Section Objectives"
    After completing this section, you will be able to:

    - Explain the statutory basis for the CFTC's cross-border reach
    - Define "U.S. person" under the CFTC's cross-border framework
    - Distinguish between direct and indirect CFTC jurisdiction over foreign entities
    - Apply the framework to common cross-border transaction scenarios

---

## Statutory Basis

CEA §2(i), added by Dodd-Frank, provides that the swap provisions of the CEA "shall not apply to activities outside the United States unless those activities have a direct and significant connection with activities in, or effect on, commerce of the United States." This language — "direct and significant" — is the hook for the CFTC's entire cross-border framework. It establishes that CFTC jurisdiction extends beyond U.S. borders, but only when the nexus to U.S. commerce is strong enough.

The CFTC's 2013 Cross-Border Guidance (78 Fed. Reg. 45292) and subsequent 2020 amendments (85 Fed. Reg. 56924) operationalized this statutory hook into a detailed framework that determines which swap transactions — and which entities — are subject to U.S. regulation regardless of where they occur.

## Who Is a "U.S. Person"?

The CFTC defines "U.S. person" more broadly than you might expect. The definition includes:

- **Natural persons resident in the United States** — straightforward; if you live in the U.S., you're a U.S. person
- **Entities organized or incorporated under U.S. law** — any LLC, corporation, LP, or trust formed in any U.S. state or territory
- **Estates of U.S. decedents and trusts governed by U.S. law**
- **Pension plans for U.S. employees** — even if the plan sponsor is a foreign entity
- **Entities majority-owned by U.S. persons** — this extends the reach to foreign entities with U.S. controlling interests
- **Entities with their principal place of business in the United States**

Critically, the definition also captures certain foreign entities through **guarantee arrangements**: a non-U.S. entity whose swap obligations are guaranteed by a U.S. person may itself be treated as subject to CFTC requirements, because the U.S. guarantor's financial risk creates the "direct and significant" connection to U.S. commerce.

## Direct vs. Indirect Reach

The CFTC's cross-border framework operates at two levels:

**Direct application (Entity-Level Requirements):** Swap dealer registration, capital requirements, risk management, and chief compliance officer requirements apply directly to any entity that meets the relevant thresholds, regardless of location, if the entity transacts with U.S. persons or conducts swap-dealing activity that has a direct and significant connection to U.S. commerce.

**Transaction-Level Requirements:** Clearing, trade execution, real-time reporting, and certain business conduct standards are applied on a per-transaction basis depending on the counterparties involved.

| Scenario | Entity-Level (SD Registration, Capital) | Transaction-Level (Clearing, Reporting) |
|----------|----------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| U.S. SD ↔ U.S. Person | Full CFTC requirements | Full CFTC requirements |
| U.S. SD ↔ Non-U.S. Person | Full CFTC requirements on the SD | Generally applicable (some substituted compliance available) |
| Non-U.S. SD (registered) ↔ U.S. Person | Full CFTC requirements (with substituted compliance for some) | Full CFTC requirements |
| Non-U.S. SD (registered) ↔ Non-U.S. Person | Substituted compliance generally available | Limited CFTC reach (primarily reporting) |
| Non-U.S. Entity (unregistered) ↔ U.S. Person | May trigger SD registration if de minimis threshold exceeded | CFTC requirements apply to the transaction |
| Non-U.S. Entity ↔ Non-U.S. Entity | Generally no CFTC jurisdiction | No CFTC jurisdiction (unless U.S. guarantee involved) |

## The Guarantee Conduit

The guarantee issue deserves special attention because it creates CFTC jurisdiction where parties might not expect it. If a non-U.S. subsidiary enters swaps and its obligations are guaranteed by a U.S. parent company, the CFTC treats those transactions as having a direct and significant connection to U.S. commerce — because the U.S. parent bears the ultimate financial risk.

This has concrete implications for global banks that operate through foreign subsidiaries with parent guarantees. JPMorgan's London branch entering swaps guaranteed by JPMorgan Chase & Co. (the U.S. parent) brings those London transactions within CFTC jurisdiction.

For DeFi, the guarantee conduit is less directly applicable — but the underlying principle is critical: **the CFTC follows the risk**. If financial risk from a swap transaction ultimately lands with a U.S. person, the CFTC asserts jurisdiction regardless of where the transaction was executed.

---

## Section Summary

- CEA §2(i) extends CFTC jurisdiction to offshore activities with a "direct and significant" U.S. nexus
- "U.S. person" is defined broadly: residents, U.S.-organized entities, entities with U.S. majority ownership, and entities with U.S. guarantors
- Entity-level requirements (registration, capital) apply to the entity; transaction-level requirements (clearing, reporting) apply per trade
- The CFTC follows the risk: guarantee arrangements by U.S. persons extend jurisdiction to foreign transactions
