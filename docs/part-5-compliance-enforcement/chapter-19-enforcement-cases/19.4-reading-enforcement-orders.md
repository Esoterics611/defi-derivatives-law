# 19.4 Reading Enforcement Orders

!!! info "Section Objectives"
    After completing this section, you will be able to:

    - Identify the structural components of a CFTC enforcement order
    - Extract actionable intelligence from findings of fact and conclusions of law
    - Interpret "neither admit nor deny" settlement language
    - Analyze what the CFTC chose NOT to charge and why it matters
    - Use AI models to systematically parse enforcement orders

## Anatomy of a CFTC Enforcement Order

Every CFTC enforcement order — whether a consent order (settlement) or a litigated judgment — follows a standard structure. Understanding this structure allows you to quickly extract the information that matters.

| Section | What It Contains | What to Look For |
|---------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Caption** | Case name, docket number, date, respondent identity | The entity type (individual, corporation, DAO); the court or administrative forum |
| **Preamble / Background** | Narrative description of the respondent's business and the conduct at issue | How the CFTC characterizes the respondent's activities — this framing reveals their legal theory |
| **Findings of Fact** | Detailed factual allegations | Specific dates, amounts, trading activity, communications, customer harm — the evidentiary basis |
| **Conclusions of Law** | Legal analysis connecting facts to CEA violations | Which CEA sections and CFTC rules are cited — this is the legal precedent |
| **Order** | Remedies imposed | Penalties, disgorgement, injunctions, trading bans, compliance undertakings |
| **Consent / Settlement Language** | "Neither admit nor deny" or other stipulations | Whether the respondent contested or cooperated; the scope of the settlement |

## Reading Between the Lines

The most valuable intelligence in a CFTC enforcement order is often what's **not** there.

**What they charged vs. what they could have charged.** In the Opyn case, the CFTC charged registration and compliance failures: an unregistered swap execution facility, unregistered futures commission merchant activity, off-exchange retail commodity transactions, and no customer identification program. It did not charge fraud or manipulation, and it named no individuals. Limiting the case to strict-liability registration failures gave the CFTC a clean statement of position (a novel token can be a swap, and collateral minting can be margin) without harder-to-prove charges.

**The factual narrative's framing.** How the CFTC describes the respondent's activities reveals their theory of the case. In the Ooki DAO order, the CFTC described the DAO as an "unincorporated association" — a deliberate legal characterization that enabled the court to treat the DAO as a legal entity. The choice of this framing, rather than piercing the veil to individual founders, established DAO liability as a precedent.

**Penalty relative to the violation.** A $250,000 penalty for operating an unregistered swaps platform (Opyn) is low. This signals either significant cooperation, mitigating factors (like small customer harm), or a strategic choice to set precedent without deterring other protocols from cooperating. By contrast, Ooki DAO's $643,542 penalty for a similar violation — combined with an injunction — signals the cost of non-cooperation.

**Compliance undertakings.** Settlement orders often include specific compliance requirements: implementing controls, retaining compliance consultants, filing reports with the CFTC. These undertakings reveal what the CFTC considers minimum compliance infrastructure for DeFi protocols.

## The "Neither Admit Nor Deny" Language

Most CFTC consent orders include the standard "neither admit nor deny" clause: the respondent settles the case without admitting or denying the allegations. This language has specific legal significance:

- The respondent avoids a formal admission that could be used against them in private litigation
- The CFTC obtains the penalties and precedent it wants without the cost of trial
- The findings of fact and conclusions of law in the order are the CFTC's positions, not admitted facts

For DeFi practitioners, "neither admit nor deny" settlements are still precedential in practice. While they are technically not binding legal precedent (no court has ruled on the merits), the CFTC treats them as establishing its enforcement position. Future respondents who engage in the same conduct will face the same charges — and the CFTC will cite the prior settlement as evidence of its consistent position.

## Practical Exercise: Parsing the Opyn Order

To illustrate, consider the key elements from the CFTC's Opyn enforcement order:

**Caption tells you:** An administrative order instituting proceedings and imposing remedial sanctions (a settled CFTC proceeding, not a federal court case), with Opyn, Inc. as respondent (corporate entity, not individuals).

**Findings of fact tell you:** Opyn's protocol offered oSQTH, a token tracking an index of ETH² against USDC. Users went long by buying oSQTH and short by depositing ETH collateral and minting it. U.S. users reached the protocol despite IP blocking.

**Conclusions of law tell you:** oSQTH tokens are swaps and leveraged or margined retail commodity transactions. Opyn operated an unregistered swap execution facility (§5h(a)(1)), acted as an unregistered FCM (§4d), offered retail commodity transactions off-exchange (§4(a)), and had no customer identification program (Regulation 42.2).

**Order tells you:** $250,000 CMP. Cease and desist from future violations. No trading ban. No individual liability.

**What's not there:** No fraud charges. No manipulation charges. No individual officer liability. No requirement to shut down entirely (just to comply going forward). This is the lightest possible enforcement outcome — signaling that Opyn cooperated extensively and the CFTC wanted the precedent without punitive consequences.

!!! info "AI Practitioner Note: Parsing Enforcement Orders with AI"
    When analyzing a CFTC enforcement order, use this prompt template:

    *"Parse the following CFTC enforcement order and extract: (1) Respondent identity and entity type, (2) All CEA sections and CFTC rules cited, (3) Each specific violation alleged with supporting facts, (4) Penalty imposed (CMPs, disgorgement, restitution, injunctions, bans), (5) Whether the order is a consent order (settlement) or litigated judgment, (6) Compliance undertakings required, (7) Precedential holdings — what legal principles does this order establish? (8) What the CFTC could have charged but didn't — and what this omission suggests about enforcement strategy. Present the analysis as a structured brief suitable for a compliance team."*

    **What to Verify:** AI models will accurately extract stated information but may miss nuance in what's omitted. Always conduct your own analysis of what's NOT in the order — the omissions are often more strategically important than the charges.

## Section Summary

- CFTC enforcement orders follow a standard structure: caption, findings of fact, conclusions of law, and order
- What the CFTC chose NOT to charge is often as revealing as what they charged — omissions signal enforcement strategy
- "Neither admit nor deny" settlements are not formal admissions but function as enforcement precedent in practice
- Penalty levels relative to the violation reveal cooperation credit and strategic enforcement choices
- AI models can effectively parse order structure but require human analysis for the strategic implications
