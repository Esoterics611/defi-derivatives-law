# 9.3 Prediction Markets & Event Contracts

!!! info "Section Objectives"
    After completing this section, you will be able to:

    - Define event contracts and their regulatory classification under the CEA
    - Explain the CFTC's authority over prediction markets and the "gaming" exclusion
    - Analyze the Polymarket settlement and Kalshi litigation as defining precedents
    - Evaluate the regulatory status of different prediction market categories

## Event Contracts Under the CEA

An event contract — sometimes called a binary option or prediction market contract — is a derivative that pays a fixed amount (typically $1) if a specified event occurs and $0 if it does not. Users buy "shares" at a price between $0 and $1, reflecting the market's assessed probability of the event occurring.

Event contracts are derivatives under the CEA. CEA §1a(47)(A)(ii) captures any agreement "that provides for any purchase, sale, payment, or delivery that is dependent on the occurrence, nonoccurrence, or the extent of an occurrence of an event or contingency associated with a potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence." A prediction market contract is precisely this: a payment dependent on the occurrence of an event.

However, the CEA places special restrictions on event contracts. Dodd-Frank added CEA §5c(c)(5)(C), which authorizes the CFTC to prohibit event contracts that involve "activity unlawful under any Federal or State law," "terrorism," "assassination," or — critically — "gaming." The CFTC can also prohibit contracts that are "contrary to the public interest."

## The Key Players

**Kalshi:** A CFTC-registered DCM that lists event contracts on economic, weather, and political events. Kalshi is the only platform that has pursued full CFTC registration for prediction market contracts. In 2023-2024, Kalshi sued the CFTC after the agency blocked its proposed election event contracts, arguing they constituted "gaming." The D.C. Circuit ruled in Kalshi's favor, allowing election contracts to trade on a registered exchange.

**Polymarket:** An unregistered prediction market operating on Polygon. Polymarket allows users to trade on a wide range of events — elections, geopolitical events, entertainment outcomes, sports — using USDC as collateral. Polymarket settled with the CFTC in 2022 for $1.4 million for offering unregistered event contracts to U.S. persons. It subsequently geo-blocked U.S. users but continued operating for non-U.S. users, experiencing massive volume during the 2024 U.S. election cycle.

**Augur:** A decentralized prediction market protocol on Ethereum using an optimistic oracle for event resolution. Augur was among the first DeFi prediction markets but never achieved significant volume. Its fully decentralized architecture makes enforcement more difficult but doesn't eliminate the underlying regulatory classification of its contracts.

## The Regulatory Classification Matrix

| Market Category | Example Contracts | CFTC Classification | Regulatory Status |
|----------------|-------------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Economic indicators** | "Will CPI exceed 3% in December?" | Event contract on economic data — clearly within CFTC jurisdiction | Kalshi lists these as a registered DCM; Polymarket offers them without registration |
| **Weather** | "Will Hurricane season 2025 produce 15+ named storms?" | Event contract on weather event — within CFTC jurisdiction (weather derivatives have traded on CME) | Generally accepted as legitimate hedging/risk transfer instruments |
| **Elections** | "Will [candidate] win the 2024 presidential election?" | Contested — CFTC argued "gaming"; D.C. Circuit sided with Kalshi that election contracts are not gaming | Permitted on registered DCMs after Kalshi ruling; unregistered platforms face enforcement |
| **Entertainment/sports** | "Will [movie] gross $500M?" / "Will [team] win the Super Bowl?" | Most likely "gaming" under §5c(c)(5)(C) — CFTC has broad authority to prohibit | High enforcement risk; sports-related contracts most likely to be classified as gaming |
| **Crypto-specific** | "Will ETH be above $5,000 on December 31?" | Event contract on commodity price — within CFTC jurisdiction | Functionally a binary option on a commodity — CFTC has enforced against unregistered binary options platforms |

## The "Gaming" Question

The CEA's prohibition on event contracts involving "gaming" was central to the CFTC's opposition to Kalshi's election contracts. The CFTC argued that election contracts are a form of political gambling — wagering on an outcome the participant cannot control, with no hedging or price discovery purpose.

Kalshi argued that election contracts serve legitimate economic functions: businesses hedge regulatory risk, investors hedge policy risk, and the markets provide valuable probability estimates that inform decision-making. The D.C. Circuit agreed with Kalshi, holding that the CFTC had not adequately justified its "gaming" characterization.

The Kalshi ruling does not resolve the gaming question for all prediction markets. Sports outcomes, entertainment results, and other "pure event" contracts remain vulnerable to the gaming exclusion. The line between "legitimate event contract" and "gambling" will be drawn case by case.

!!! warning "The Unresolved Tension"
    Prediction markets occupy an uncomfortable regulatory space: they are simultaneously derivatives (subject to CFTC regulation) and resemble gambling (subject to state gambling laws). The CFTC has primary jurisdiction as a derivatives regulator, but state attorneys general could independently pursue prediction market operators under state gambling statutes. A prediction market platform could face simultaneous federal derivatives enforcement and state gambling prosecution for the same activity.

## Section Summary

- Event contracts are derivatives under CEA §1a(47)(A)(ii), with special restrictions under §5c(c)(5)(C)
- The CFTC can prohibit event contracts involving gaming, terrorism, or activity contrary to the public interest
- Kalshi operates as a registered DCM; Polymarket settled with the CFTC in 2022 for $1.4M for operating without registration
- The Kalshi ruling permits election contracts on registered exchanges but does not resolve the gaming question for all event categories
- Prediction markets face overlapping federal derivatives and state gambling jurisdiction
