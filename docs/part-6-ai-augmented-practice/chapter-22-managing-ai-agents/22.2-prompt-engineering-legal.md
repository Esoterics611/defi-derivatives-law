# 22.2 Prompt Engineering for Legal Domains

!!! info "Section Objectives"
    After completing this section, you will be able to:

    - Construct legal system prompts that establish jurisdiction, applicable law, and role definition
    - Design structured output formats for legal memos, compliance checklists, and risk matrices
    - Apply chain-of-thought reasoning patterns specific to legal analysis
    - Implement the adversarial reviewer pattern for self-checking AI legal output
    - Avoid the anti-patterns that produce confident but legally wrong results

---

## Why Legal Prompt Engineering Is Different

Generic prompt engineering teaches you to be clear and specific. Legal prompt engineering demands something more: **precision that carries liability consequences**. When an AI generates marketing copy with a slight inaccuracy, you lose credibility. When an AI generates a legal analysis with a slight inaccuracy, your client may violate federal law.

Three properties distinguish legal prompts:

1. **Jurisdictional specificity** — "US law" is not specific enough. You need "CFTC regulations under the Commodity Exchange Act, as amended by Dodd-Frank Title VII, applying current rules as of [date]."
2. **Citation requirements** — Legal analysis without citations is opinion. Every claim must reference a statute, rule, case, or regulatory guidance.
3. **Temporal precision** — Law changes. A prompt must establish the relevant date and instruct the AI to flag any uncertainty about whether a cited authority is current.

## The Legal System Prompt

Every legal agent needs a system prompt that establishes five elements:

```
You are a [role] specializing in [domain].

JURISDICTION: [Applicable law — e.g., United States federal law, specifically
the Commodity Exchange Act (7 U.S.C. §§ 1-27f) and CFTC regulations
(17 CFR Parts 1-200)]

TEMPORAL CONTEXT: Analyze based on law and regulations in effect as of
[date]. Flag any citations where you are uncertain about current validity.

CITATION STANDARD: Every legal conclusion must cite the specific statutory
section, regulatory provision, or enforcement precedent. Use format:
[statute/rule number] — [brief description]. Do NOT cite cases or
authorities you cannot verify exist.

OUTPUT CONSTRAINTS: [Structured format specification — see below]

LIMITATIONS DISCLOSURE: If you are uncertain about any legal conclusion,
state your uncertainty explicitly. Never present uncertain analysis as
definitive. If asked about a topic outside your specified jurisdiction,
decline and explain why.
```

## Structured Output Formats

Unstructured legal analysis from AI tends toward verbose, hedged prose that buries the conclusion. Force structured output:

### Legal Memo Structure

| Section | Content | AI Instruction |
|---------|---------|---------------|
| **Issue** | One-sentence statement of the legal question | "State the precise legal question in one sentence" |
| **Short Answer** | Direct answer — yes/no/likely/unlikely with qualification | "Provide a direct answer before analysis" |
| **Applicable Law** | Statutes, rules, and precedents that govern | "List every applicable authority with full citation" |
| **Analysis** | Application of law to facts, element-by-element | "Analyze each element of the applicable test against the facts provided" |
| **Counterarguments** | Arguments against the conclusion | "Identify the strongest argument against your conclusion" |
| **Conclusion** | Final assessment with confidence level | "State conclusion and rate confidence: high / medium / low" |

### Compliance Checklist Structure

```
For each regulatory requirement:
1. Requirement: [cite specific rule]
2. Applicable? [Yes/No — explain why]
3. Current status: [Compliant / Non-compliant / Partially compliant / Unknown]
4. Evidence: [What demonstrates compliance or non-compliance]
5. Gap: [What is missing, if anything]
6. Remediation: [Specific steps to achieve compliance]
7. Priority: [Critical / High / Medium / Low]
8. Deadline: [If any regulatory deadline applies]
```

### Risk Assessment Matrix

```
For each identified risk:
1. Risk description: [Specific regulatory risk]
2. Probability: [High / Medium / Low — with reasoning]
3. Impact: [Severe / Significant / Moderate / Minor — with reasoning]
4. Risk score: [Probability × Impact]
5. Mitigant: [Existing controls or available mitigations]
6. Residual risk: [Risk remaining after mitigants]
7. Owner: [Who is responsible for managing this risk]
```

## Chain-of-Thought for Legal Reasoning

Legal reasoning follows a specific analytical structure. Prompt the AI to follow it explicitly:

```
Analyze this issue using the following legal reasoning framework:

Step 1: IDENTIFY the applicable statute or regulation. Quote the relevant
text. If multiple provisions apply, list all of them.

Step 2: PARSE the legal test. What elements must be satisfied? What
definitions apply? Are there exceptions or safe harbors?

Step 3: APPLY each element to the specific facts. For each element, state
whether it is met, not met, or uncertain — and explain why.

Step 4: CONSIDER counterarguments. What is the strongest argument that
the opposite conclusion is correct? How would a CFTC enforcement
attorney argue? How would defense counsel respond?

Step 5: CONCLUDE with your assessment and confidence level.
```

This mirrors the IRAC (Issue-Rule-Application-Conclusion) framework taught in every American law school — and it forces the AI to show its reasoning at each step rather than leaping to a conclusion.

| Legal Task | Prompt Structure | Key Instructions | Output Format | Common Failure Mode |
|-----------|-----------------|------------------|--------------|-------------------|
| Regulatory classification | System prompt + fact pattern | "Classify under CEA §1a definitions" | Classification memo with element analysis | Conclusory classification without element-by-element analysis |
| Contract review | System prompt + document text | "Extract and analyze all non-standard provisions" | Structured extraction table + risk flags | Missing cross-references between document sections |
| Enforcement risk assessment | System prompt + protocol description | "Compare against all relevant enforcement precedents" | Risk matrix with precedent citations | Citing settled orders as if they establish binding legal precedent |
| Compliance gap analysis | System prompt + program description + rules | "Assess compliance with each applicable rule" | Compliance checklist with gap identification | Over-reporting compliance; missing gaps in operational implementation |
| Cross-border analysis | System prompt + multi-jurisdiction facts | "Compare requirements and identify conflicts" | Comparison table with conflict flags | Conflating EU and US regulatory concepts with similar names |

## The Adversarial Reviewer Pattern

The single most effective quality improvement for AI legal output: have a second AI instance critique the first.

```
REVIEWER SYSTEM PROMPT:
You are a senior derivatives regulatory attorney reviewing a junior
associate's legal analysis. Your job is to find errors, weaknesses,
and unsupported conclusions.

Review the following legal analysis and identify:
1. Any legal citations that may be incorrect, outdated, or fabricated
2. Any logical gaps in the reasoning — places where the conclusion
   does not follow from the analysis
3. Any missing authorities — statutes, rules, or precedents that
   should have been considered
4. Any factual assumptions that are stated but not verified
5. Any hedging language that masks analytical uncertainty ("may,"
   "could potentially," "it is arguable") — demand a clear position
6. Rate overall quality: Accept / Revise / Reject
```

The adversarial reviewer catches 40-60% of substantive errors that a single-pass generation misses. It is not a substitute for human review — but it dramatically improves the quality of what humans receive.

## Anti-Patterns: What NOT to Do

!!! danger "AI Limitation Warning"
    **The most dangerous AI output is a well-structured legal memo with fabricated case citations.** It looks authoritative. It follows the right format. The reasoning is plausible. But the cases it cites may not exist, and the holdings it attributes to real cases may be invented. Every citation in AI-generated legal output must be independently verified.

Common prompt anti-patterns that produce dangerous output:

1. **"Provide a comprehensive legal analysis"** — too vague. The AI will fill space with plausible-sounding but unverifiable claims. Be specific about scope.
2. **"What are the legal risks?"** — no jurisdiction, no applicable law, no fact pattern. You'll get generic risk factors, not actionable analysis.
3. **"Draft a legal opinion"** — legal opinions carry professional liability. AI should draft memos and analyses, never opinions.
4. **"Cite relevant case law"** — an invitation to hallucinate. Instead: "Cite the specific statutory section and CFTC rule. Only cite cases if you are certain they exist and can provide the full case name, court, year, and reporter citation."
5. **Omitting the date context** — AI may cite superseded rules or overruled holdings without realizing the law has changed.
