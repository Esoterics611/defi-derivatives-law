# 14.5 Bankruptcy Safe Harbors & DeFi Insolvency

!!! info "Section Objectives"
    After completing this section, you will be able to:

    - Identify the key U.S. Bankruptcy Code safe harbors for derivatives
    - Explain why safe harbors exist and what they protect
    - Analyze the applicability of bankruptcy safe harbors to DeFi protocols
    - Assess the DeFi insolvency problem using Terra/Luna as a case study

---

## The Problem Safe Harbors Solve

When a company files for bankruptcy, the **automatic stay** (11 U.S.C. §362) immediately halts all collection actions, lawsuits, and enforcement of liens against the debtor. This is fundamental bankruptcy policy — it gives the debtor breathing room to reorganize or liquidate in an orderly fashion.

For derivatives, the automatic stay creates a catastrophic problem. If a counterparty defaults on a derivatives portfolio and the non-defaulting party cannot immediately close out and net the positions, the surviving party is left with unhedged market risk that compounds every hour the stay remains in effect. A $50 million net exposure on Monday can become a $200 million loss by Friday if the market moves.

Congress recognized this risk and created **safe harbors** that exempt derivatives from the automatic stay.

## The Four Key Safe Harbors

| Provision | What It Protects | Conditions |
|-----------|-----------------|------------|
| **§362(b)(17)** | Right to **offset or net** mutual debts under swap agreements, despite the automatic stay | Must qualify as a "swap participant" with a "swap agreement" as defined in §101 |
| **§546(g)** | Protects **margin transfers** from avoidance as preferential transfers | Transfer must be a "margin payment" or "settlement payment" under a swap agreement |
| **§560** | Right to exercise **contractual close-out rights** under a swap agreement | Non-defaulting party can terminate and liquidate positions without court approval |
| **§561** | Right to exercise **netting rights** under a "master netting agreement" | Protects the single-agreement netting structure of ISDA Master Agreements |

### Scope: "Swap Agreement" Definition

The Bankruptcy Code's definition of "swap agreement" (11 U.S.C. §101(53B)) is deliberately broad. It covers interest rate swaps, currency swaps, commodity swaps, equity swaps, credit default swaps, total return swaps, and — critically — "any agreement or transaction that is similar to any other agreement or transaction referred to in this paragraph and that is of a type that has been, is presently, or in the future becomes, the subject of recurrent dealing in the swap or other derivatives markets."

This forward-looking language was designed to capture financial innovation. The question for DeFi: does a perpetual futures contract on dYdX, or a liquidity provision position on Uniswap, qualify as a "swap agreement" under this definition?

### Scope: "Swap Participant" Definition

A "swap participant" (11 U.S.C. §101(53C)) is an entity that "at any time before the filing of the petition, has an outstanding swap agreement with the debtor." This definition is entity-based — it requires an identifiable counterparty. In DeFi, where positions are often against a liquidity pool rather than a named counterparty, the concept strains.

## Application to DeFi: Three Fundamental Gaps

**Gap 1: No identifiable counterparty.** Safe harbors protect the rights of a "swap participant" — a specific entity with a contractual relationship to the debtor. When a DeFi user trades against an automated market maker (AMM), who is the counterparty? The protocol? The liquidity providers? The DAO? The smart contract itself? Without a clear counterparty, there is no "swap participant" to invoke safe harbor protections.

**Gap 2: No contractual agreement.** Safe harbors protect rights under a "swap agreement" — a contract between parties. DeFi positions are governed by smart contract code, not bilateral legal agreements. A user interacting with Aave's lending protocol has not signed an ISDA Master Agreement or anything resembling one. Whether smart contract interactions constitute "agreements" under the Bankruptcy Code is an open legal question.

**Gap 3: Smart contract self-help vs. legal process.** DeFi protocols enforce their own "close-out" through automated liquidation — when collateral falls below the maintenance threshold, anyone can trigger a liquidation and seize the collateral. This self-help mechanism operates without regard to legal proceedings. If a DeFi user files for bankruptcy, their on-chain collateral may be liquidated by a bot within minutes, before any court could impose a stay. The question then becomes: was that liquidation a valid exercise of safe harbor rights, or a violation of the automatic stay?

| Safe Harbor Element | TradFi Implementation | DeFi Reality | Legal Gap |
|--------------------|----------------------|--------------|-----------|
| Swap agreement | ISDA Master Agreement + Confirmations | Smart contract code + transaction parameters | No legal certainty that smart contract interactions are "agreements" |
| Swap participant | Named counterparty with LEI | Pseudonymous wallet address, LP pool, or DAO | No identifiable "participant" in many cases |
| Close-out right | Section 6 Early Termination notice | Automated liquidation by any keeper/bot | Self-help execution may violate automatic stay |
| Netting right | Section 2(c) payment netting, Section 6(e) close-out netting | No cross-protocol netting mechanism | Each protocol position is legally independent |
| Margin transfer protection | CSA-governed collateral movement | Smart contract collateral deposits/withdrawals | Unclear if on-chain collateral movements qualify |

## Case Study: Terra/Luna — Unstructured Insolvency

In May 2022, the Terra/Luna ecosystem collapsed, destroying approximately $40 billion in value. The collapse of TerraUSD (UST) and its sister token LUNA represented the closest thing DeFi has experienced to a major derivatives counterparty failure.

**What happened in TradFi terms:** A massive counterparty (the Terra protocol) experienced the equivalent of a credit event. Every position referencing UST or LUNA — lending positions on Anchor, liquidity positions on Astroport, derivative positions on Mirror — was suddenly underwater. In TradFi, this would trigger ISDA Events of Default across thousands of Master Agreements, followed by orderly close-out netting protected by bankruptcy safe harbors.

**What actually happened:** Chaos. There was no orderly close-out process. No netting. No safe harbor protections. Liquidation bots seized collateral from users across protocols. Some users lost collateral that, under an orderly close-out regime, would have been returned. Others maintained profitable positions that a bankruptcy trustee might have clawed back. The result was a zero-recovery, first-come-first-served liquidation with no legal framework to allocate losses fairly.

**The lesson:** The absence of bankruptcy-like protections in DeFi doesn't mean there are no insolvencies — it means insolvencies are resolved by code speed rather than legal process. Sophisticated actors with faster bots and better MEV strategies extract value, while retail users bear disproportionate losses. This is precisely the outcome that the TradFi bankruptcy safe harbor framework was designed to prevent.

!!! danger ""
    **The DeFi Insolvency Paradox:** DeFi protocols can "fail" in ways that look exactly like derivatives counterparty defaults — but with no bankruptcy court, no orderly close-out, no netting, and no safe harbors. The result is that losses are allocated by transaction speed rather than legal priority. This is a feature of the technology and a failure of the legal framework.

Consider: (1) whether the protocol interaction creates a bilateral 'agreement,' (2) whether there is an identifiable 'swap participant' counterparty, (3) whether the position is 'similar to' enumerated swap types, and (4) whether the forward-looking language ('in the future becomes the subject of recurrent dealing') could encompass this instrument."*

    **What to Verify:** AI models will often confidently state that DeFi positions "likely qualify" under the broad statutory language. Push back — the counterparty identification problem and the agreement formation question are genuinely unsettled, and no court has ruled on these issues.

## Section Summary

- U.S. Bankruptcy Code safe harbors (§§362(b)(17), 546(g), 560, 561) protect derivatives counterparties' rights to close out and net positions despite the automatic stay
- The broad "swap agreement" definition may capture some DeFi instruments, but the counterparty identification and agreement formation problems create fundamental gaps
- DeFi protocols enforce their own "close-out" through automated liquidation, which may or may not constitute valid safe harbor exercises
- Terra/Luna demonstrated the consequences of unstructured DeFi insolvency: losses allocated by bot speed, not legal priority
